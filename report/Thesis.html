Abstract

Funding agencies rely on expert evaluations to assess the quality of research proposals submitted to a funding call. Most often, experts are asked to score proposals on an ordinal or continuous scale. Some funders instead ask experts to rank the proposals from best to worst, and few funders ask experts to both score and rank proposals. These scoring and ranking data are then statistically analysed by the funders in order to take funding decisions.

This work formalises the concepts of ranking and scoring data in the context of grant proposal quality assessment. We review and compare six different frequentist and Bayesian methods that can be used in the analysis of scoring and/or ranking data. We apply these methods on a real-world dataset containing both scoring and ranking data collected by the American Institute for Biological sciences. We also present and compare two different methods for posterior distribution estimation in the Bayesian models: the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (), and a Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation ().

In the data analysis, each of the six models used provides a different ordering of the grant proposals. The choice of model thus has a direct impact on the assessment of proposal quality, and, depending on the amount of proposals to be funded, a direct impact on the funding decision. The choice of Bayesian posterior distribution estimation method did not yield any difference in the results. However, the implementation is significantly faster to fit than the simulation.

We show and discuss that some methods for ranking data favour proposals that consistently obtain good ranks, while others favour proposals which polarise and have both very high or very low ranks. These findings are currently limited to the dataset analysed, and the analyses we performed need to be replicated on simulation data and other real-world data in order to provide robust recommendations. We also propose possible approaches to combine scoring and ranking data in joint models in order to combine the information from both data types. While these joint approaches have yet to be implemented, we hypothesise that they could provide more precise and fair analyses than separate models for scores and ranks. This work highlights the importance of the choice of statistical model in the assessment of grant proposal quality, and provides a solid foundation for further work in this field.

Introduction

Grant Proposal Funding

When resources are limited, decisions have to be made on how to allocate them. In research, funding agencies make these decisions by accepting or rejecting grant proposals. Agencies contact panels of experts which individually review, rank or score proposals, the best of which are funded and the rest rejected (Harman 1998). However, there is evidence for bias in this process. For instance, funding for innovative, risky or interdisciplinary research is less likely to be awarded than for conservative and conventional proposals (Guthrie et al. 2019). Moreover, evaluations from individual experts are known to be unreliable, even after panel meetings, making it hard to objectively assess which proposals are worth funding (Fogelholm et al. 2012). Despite these shortcomings, there is no existing alternative to this expert peer review system. To mitigate its downsides, statistical methods can be used to analyse the experts’ evaluations in order to ensure fair decision making.

Depending on the assessment system, the experts either make qualitative or quantitative statements about the quality of the proposals. This project will focus on statistical methods to analyse two quantitative expert evaluation systems:

To evaluate grant proposal qualities from these data, multiple statistical models, both frequentist and Bayesian, have already been developed. In this thesis, we propose a novel unified notation framework to write about these models. Using this framework, we review and reproduce six statistical methods for evaluating scoring data and ranking data. We then compare the results provided by each model and discuss their implications. Finally, we provide multiple paths that can be taken towards creating a joint model combining scoring and ranking data to provide quality estimates. We hypothesise that a joint model would enable a more precise analysis of object quality in comparison to the other models made for this purpose. This would improve the current decision-making process and ultimately allow funding agencies to allocate their resources more fairly.

Definitions

Many different notations and formulations are used in literature to refer to ranking and scoring data. For clarity, this section defines the notation that we will use in this thesis. It is inspired by Heyard et al. (2022) and Xia (2019).

Objects and Assessors

We define objects as the set of items that are ranked and scored by the assessors. Conversely, we define assessors as the people giving scores and rankings to the objects. In this thesis, the objects will be the grant proposals and the assessors the experts on the evaluation panel.

NoteDefinitions: Objects and Assessors

Objects are a set of \(n \ \epsilon \ \mathbb{N}\) items to be ranked or scored by assessors. In this thesis we will denote the set of objects as \(\mathcal{O} = \{o_1, ..., o_n\}\), with a subscript \(i \ \epsilon \{1,...,n\}\).

Assessors are a set of \(m \in \mathbb{N}\) experts who give scores and ranks to the objects \(\mathcal{O}\). We will denote the individual assessors with a subscript \(j \ \epsilon \{1,...,m\}\).

Scores

Scores are obtained by having assessors assign a grade to each object by estimating its quality. Grades are measured on continuous or ordinal scales of different lengths. When scoring, assessors examine each object individually and compare it to their personal representation of an ideal object; their assessment of each object is assumed to be independent from all other objects (see Figure \(\ref{fig-rankscore}\), left). In this thesis, we work with scoring data on a continuous scale and we use the convention that .  

NoteDefinition: Scores

Scores \(S_j\) are a set of grades given to all of \(\mathcal{O}\) by an assessor \(j\). The score given to a specific object \(o_i \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{O}\) by a specific assessor \(j\) will be written \(y_{i,j}\) with \(i \ \epsilon \ \{1,...,n\}\) and \(j \ \epsilon \ \{1, ..., m\}\).

Scoring data \(S = \{S_1, ..., S_m\}\) are the observed scores given by all assessors.

Rankings

In contrast to scores, rankings are obtained by having each assessor directly compare all objects against each other and order them from best to worst. The objects are therefore assessed on a relative scale; the assessment of each object is directly influenced by the quality of all other objects in the objects (see Figure \(\ref{fig-rankscore}\), right). In this thesis, we will use the convention that .

NoteDefinitions: Rankings

Linear orders \(\mathbf{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})}\) are the set of all possible ranking orders of \(\mathcal{O}\). For any pair of objects \(o_i\), \(o_l\) \(\in \mathcal{O}\), we will denote \(o_i\) \(\succ\) \(o_l\) if \(o_i\) is preferred to \(o_l\).

Ranking order \(R_j \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\) is the linear order given by a specific assessors \(j \ \epsilon \ \{1,...,m\}\) to \(\mathcal{O}\). The rank position given to an object \(o_i \in \mathcal{O}\) by an assessor \(j\) will be denoted \(r_{i,j}\).

Ranking data \(R = \{R_1, ..., R_m\}\) are the observed ranking orders given by all assessors.

Object Quality and Final Ordering

The ultimate objective of our modelling will be to select the most appropriate subset of objects for funding, given the available funding amount. To obtain this subset, we will need to make a final ordering of the objects, sorting the objects from best to worst. This is either achieved, in models estimating a latent quality variable, by sorting the latent quality variable in ascending order (lower values of the latent quality variables represent higher object qualities), or, in models which directly calculate a ranking, by taking a model’s predicted true underlying ranking. Once the final ordering is achieved, a funding line can be introduced. The funding line represents the highest value of the latent quality (the minimal quality standard) necessary for an object to be funded, or, in the case where only a final ordering is available, the limit rank an object needs to achieve in order to be funded.

NoteDefinitions: Quality and Final Ordering

Object latent quality \(\theta^{k}_i\) is the latent quality of an object \(o_i\) according to a model \(k\).

Final ordering \(\mathcal{F}_k(\mathcal{O}) \ \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\) is the final ordering of the objects \(\mathcal{O}\) according to model \(k\).

Funding line In the cases where \(\theta^{k}_i\) is available, the highest value of the latent quality \(\theta^{k}_i\) possible for an object to be funded. In the cases where only \(\mathcal{F}_k(\mathcal{O})\) is available, the limit rank an object needs to reach in order for it to be funded.

Modelling Approaches to Scoring Data and Ranking Data

Models for scoring data are very abundant in literature. In general, it is assumed that the scores given to objects follow a given distribution from which parameters can be inferred. By interpreting these parameters, statements can be made about the scoring data (for example, regarding the spread of the scores or the expected values of the scores for specific objects). As ranking data are in a special format, they require extra consideration when being analysed. Consequently, many different formulations and approaches exist to model them. In this section, we will discuss notions of how ranking data can be thought of, described and modelled.

Pairwise Comparisons and Ranking

Ranking data can be thought of as a series of pairwise comparisons. This approach considers that the assessors simply compare many pairs of objects and generate a ranking from these comparisons (Marden 2014). The ranking data are then modelled through a series of parameters representing the probability of winning these pairwise comparisons. This description of ranking data is used in models such as the Bradley-Terry model (Bradley and Terry 1952) and the Plackett-Luce model (Plackett 1975). This description of ranks comes at a cost as the number of parameters increases rapidly for larger ranking models.

Distance-Based Ranking

In distance-based ranking, we assume that the data have a true underlying ranking, and that the assessors are imperfect judges who attempt to reproduce this ranking (Fligner and Verducci 1986). The true ranking is perceived as a ranking order \(R^* \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\), and the further the observed ranking from this true ranking, the less likely the ranking is to be present in the data. There are many possible parametrisations of this distance. Using a distance-based model instead of a pairwise comparison model drastically reduces the amount of parameters to estimate when modelling.

Thurstonian Ranking

Thurstonian ranking is another way to model ranking data. As in distance-based ranking, we assume that the assessors are imperfect judges. However, in opposition to distance-based ranking, the ranking data are not assumed to be generated based on a true underlying linear order. Instead, we assume the ranking data to be generated by a series of latent random variables representing each object’s quality (Thurstone 1927). According to Thurstonian ranking, each assessor observes a realisation of all the random variables and ranks them from highest to lowest based on their value (see Figure \(\ref{fig-thurst}\)).

Data

To compare and reproduce models, we will fit them on a dataset made available in Gallo et al. (2023). The data were collected in the context of a real-world call for proposals by the American Institute of Biological Science (AIBS) in 2020. This dataset contains both scoring data and partial ranking data given to 28 objects (grant proposals) by 10 assessors (experts). The partial ranking is provided for the six best objects according to each assessor and the objects are scored on a scale from 1 (best) to 5 (worst). The funding was awarded to the top 3 objects. A summary of the main properties of the data can be found in Table \(\ref{tab-datov}\).

The data are unfortunately incomplete. In the scoring data there are 21 missing observations out of the 280 observations. Five of these observations are missing due to conflicts of interest (MCI) between the assessors and the object, and 16 of them are assumed to be missing completely at random (MCAR). In the ranking data, 11 observations are missing, five MCI and six MCAR. Specific considerations taken to work around the missingness in the scoring data and ranking data are discussed in their respective sections below.

Scoring Data

An overview of the scoring data can be found in Figure \(\ref{fig:expl}\). The inter-assessor reliability calculated using a two-way, mixed effect, fixed rater ICC is 0.91 (95% CI from 0.87 to 0.96), which signifies that the grades given by the assessors have a good to excellent agreement (Koo and Li 2016). In the exploratory analysis, we found a significant digit preference for the score 3, and no score worse than (higher than) 4 was given to any of the objects being evaluated. Nevertheless, this digit preference and the lack of very high scores will not affect our analysis, as none of the top contenders for the grant allocation have scores higher than \(2.5\).

There are 21 missing data points in the scoring data. This represents \(7.5\ \%\) of the data, with no single object having more than three missing scores. The MCAR data, despite increasing the variability in object quality estimation, do not create any systematic bias (Dey et al. 2026). The methods we use on scoring data in this thesis have no problems handling missing values and there is therefore little interest in imputating MCAR datapoints. On the other hand, the MCI data are intentionally not collected from the assessor because they are known to be biased. Accordingly, imputating these values is also undesirable. We can therefore analyse the scoring data without imputating nor modifying the scoring data.

Ranking Data

An overview of the ranking data can be found in Figure \(\ref{fig:rank_expl}\). Each assessor reported their prefered top 6 objects. Thus, we obtain information about the ranking on seven levels: ranks one through six, and a rank greater or equal to seven. Assessors were not allowed to consider ties in the positions one to six. The inter-assessor reliability calculated using a two-way, mixed effect, fixed rater ICC is 0.55 (95% CI from 0.33 to 0.8), which signifies that the grades given by the assessors have a moderate level of agreement (Koo and Li 2016).

There are 11 missing data points in the ranking data, five MCI, and six MCAR, which represent \(18.3\%\) of the ranking data. The MCAR data are due to an assessor ignoring the ranking task. We remove them from the dataset as they do not provide any information about the object qualities. However, due to the nature of the models we are using, the MCI data are problematic. The models assume that when an object has a missing value, it did not make it into the top six of an assessor. Reformulated, a missing value given by a specific assessor represents an object quality worse than the 6th ranked object according to them. However, in the case of MCI data, the missing values do not provide any information about the quality of the object. Therefore, there is a need for data imputation.

Upon further inspection of the data, three of the five MCI datapoints in this dataset represent objects that were ranked in position seven or greater by all assessors. Moreover, the two last MCI datapoints, were each only included in one of the nine assessor’s top six and given the sixth rank. Given this information, we consider that the MCI datapoints represent object which would have been of worse quality than the assessors top-six’s, which removes the need for imputation. This can only be assumed due to the nature of this dataset, and would have to be done differently on other datasets.

Two of these methods are focused on analysing scoring data, three of them on ranking data and one of them jointly models scoring and ranking data. Models using Scoring Data

Mean Scores

A first and straightforward approach proposed by Zehlike et al. (2022) to assess the quality of each object is to use the mean of all the scores \(y_{ij}\) obtained by an object \(o_i\) as an approximation of the object’s quality. In this case, the latent quality of an object \(\theta_i^{\ mean}\) is estimated as follows:

\[\begin{gather*} \bar{y}_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^m y_{ij}}{m}, \quad i = 1, ..., n \\ \bar{y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \bar{y}_i}{n} \\ \hat{\theta}_i^{\ mean} = \bar{y}_i - \bar{y} \end{gather*}\]

Note that, by definition, high quality objects will have negative values of latent quality \(\hat{\theta}_i^{\ mean}\). This model relies on many strong assumptions. First, it assumes that each assessor has the exact same scoring habits, and is well calibrated with themselves and the rest of the group (for example, that no assessor is stricter or more lenient than their peers). Second, it also assumes that each object is evaluated completely independently from all other objects. Moreover, unless the number of assessors is extremely large, it cannot be used to provide any reasonable uncertainty estimates in its results (Kaplan et al. 2008). In this thesis, we fit this model using a homemade script (R Core Team 2026).

The mean scores model provides an estimate of the latent quality \(\hat\theta_{i}^{\ mean}\) of the objects from the scoring data. Figure \(\ref{fig:mm}\) shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. The mean score model would accept objects x17, x19, and x25. Object x19 and x25 are tied for second place, and the difference in latent quality between the tied second place and the fourth place object is very low. The exact latent quality values for the ten best objects accoding to this model are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:mm}\).

Hierarchical Model for Scores

To alleviate the assessor calibration assumptions discussed in Section 3.1.1, Heyard et al. (2022) proposed a hierarchical model for scores that includes parameters to model the different scoring behaviors of the assessors. This model still offers a latent object quality variable \(\theta_i^{\ hier}\) based on the mean of the scores obtained by each object. We defined a slightly modified version of this model as follows:

\[\begin{gather*} y_{ij} | \theta_i^{\ hier}, \lambda_{ij} \sim N(\mu + \theta_i^{\ hier} + \lambda_{j}\ , \tau_y^2) \\ \mu \sim N(0, 100) \quad \quad \theta_i^{\ hier} \sim N(0, \tau^2_{\theta}) \quad \quad \lambda_{j} \sim N(0, \tau_\lambda^2) \\ \tau_y \sim G(1.5,1.5), \quad \quad \tau_{\theta} \sim G(1.5,1.5) \quad \quad \tau_\lambda \sim G(1.5,1.5) \end{gather*}\]

With \(\mu\) taking the value of the mean score of all objects, and \(\lambda_{j}\) being the assessor-specific effect representing the differences in scoring habits and strictness. Given the scores are grades given between one and five, we fix gamma priors for the standard deviations \(\tau\) with distributions which lie within plausible values for these parameters, with modes of \(1\), and standard deviations of \(2/3\). Using this model, we obtain and extract the posterior distribution for the latent quality of each object \(\theta_i^{\ hier}\), which we use to provide credible intervals of \(\theta_i^{\ hier}\). This model makes use of the funding line, Heyard et al. (2022) propose that all proposals that have a 50% credible interval of \(\theta_i^{\ hier}\) crossing the funding line should be considered in a lottery instead of being directly accepted or rejected.

This model can be fitted using the package (Heyard 2026). This implementation uses the (Plummer 2023) framework in order to generate a sample of the posterior distributions of \(\theta_i^{\ hier}\). To fit the model, we will use both a reimplementation of this model in , and a new implementation of this model using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation framework using the (Rue et al. 2009). More information on both these methods can be found in Annex \(\ref{mcmc}\) and Annex \(\ref{inla}\). The model was called with 10000 iterations of adaptive sampling, 10000 iteration of burn-in and 80000 iterations of sampling in combination with a thinning of 20. Convergence diagnostics of the model are available in Annex \(\ref{coda-hier}\) and the model calls are available in Annex \(\ref{mod_calls}\).

Both fitting methods provide very similar posterior distribution for \(\theta_i^{hier}\). Figure \(\ref{fig:jags_inla_hier}\) shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to both the fit and the fit. Both fits would suggest a lottery to allocate the funding to three of the four best objects: x17, x25, x19 and x21. The exact latent quality values for the ten best objects according to both these fits are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:hier_m}\). Both model fits provide the same output; the largest discrepancy between the two fitting procedure is of a magnitude of \(0.02\). However, the model does not require any convergence diagnostic and is thus faster to fit.

Models using Ranking Data

Plackett-Luce

The Plackett-Luce model is a model based on pairwise comparison. It assumes that each object \(o_i \in \mathcal{O}\) has a fixed probability of coming in first place when compared with the other objects in the set \(\mathcal{O'} = \mathcal{O} \setminus \{o_i\}\) (Plackett 1975). This probability is estimated by assigning a weight \(w_i \in \mathcal{W}\) for each object \(o_i\). The higher the value of the weight, the more preferred the object. The relationship between the probability of a specific object \(o_{l}\) coming in first place and the weights \(\mathcal{W}\) is defined as follows:

\[\begin{gather} \mathbb{P}[r_{l} = 1| \mathcal{W}] = \frac{w_{l}}{\sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_i} \label{decision-luce} \end{gather}\]

The Plackett-Luce model considers each ranking order \(R_j\) as a sequence of decisions made using Equation (\(\ref{decision-luce}\)). A formulation of the likelihood of this model can be found in Annex \(\ref{lik-Luce}\). In this method, the weights \(\mathcal{W}\) are the quality estimates of the objects. In order to follow the convention that lower latent quality estimates represent higher scores, we will transform them as follows: \[\begin{gather*} \theta_i^{\ pl-lu} = (1 - w_i) \end{gather*}\] We then estimate the final ordering of the objects by sorting the weights \(\mathcal{W}\) in ascending order. To estimate the weight parameters, we fit a Plackett-Luce model using an implementation by Turner et al. (Turner et al. 2020).

The Plackett-Luce model provides an estimate of the latent quality \(\hat\theta_{i}^{\ pl-luce}\) of each object from the ranking data. Figure \(\ref{fig:pl}\) shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the three objects; x25, x19, and x21. The difference in latent quality between the third place and the fourth place object is very low. The exact latent quality values are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:pl}\).

Mallows

The Mallows model is a distance-based ranking method which considers a true underlying ranking order \(R^* \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\) and assumes the assessors are imperfect judges trying to reproduce this ranking (Mallows 1957). The probability of observing a specific ranking order \(R_j \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\) is defined as:

\[\begin{gather*} \mathbb{P}[R_j| R^* , \eta] = \frac{\exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*))}{\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)} \\ d(R_j, R^* ) = \sum_{i,l = 1}^n \mathbb{1}\{o_i \ \ {\succ}_{R_j} \ \ o_l \quad \text{and} \quad o_l \ \ {\succ}_{R^*} \ \ o_i\} \\ \psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta) = \sum_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})} \exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*)) \end{gather*}\]

where \(\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)\) is a normalizing constant, and \(d(\cdot,\ \cdot)\) is the Kendall distance (minimum sum of adjacent swaps) between two linear orders. The notation \(o_i \ \ {\succ}_{R_j} \ \ o_l\) indicates that the object \(o_i\) is ranked higher than \(o_l\) in the true ranking order \(R_j\). Conversely, \(o_l \ \ {\succ}_{R^*} \ \ o_i\) indicates that \(o_l\) is ranked higher than \(o_i\) in the true ranking order \(R^*\). The \(\eta\) parameter represents a measure of similarity between the objects \(\mathcal{O}\). A large \(\eta\) implies that the distribution of the ranking data \(R\) is closely concentrated around the true underlying rank \(R^*\), while an \(\eta\) of 0 implies that the distribution of the ranking data \(R\) is uniform across the linear orders \(\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\). Using this method, the final ordering of the objects \(\mathcal{F}_{mlw}(\mathcal{O})\) is \(\hat{R^*}\). To fit the Mallows model, we will use an implementation by Sorensen et al. (2020).

The Mallows model provides an estimate of the true underlying ranking \(\hat{R}^{*\ mlw}\) of the objects from the ranking data. Figure \(\ref{fig:Mallows}\) shows the rank estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the objects x17, x25, and x19. The exact ranking values are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:Mallows}\).

Thurstonian Model

In Thurstonian modelling, the ranking order is assumed to be determined by drawing from latent random variables \(z_{i}\) (as discussed in Section 1.3.3). The ranking orders \(R_j\) given by the assessors \(j = \{1, ..., m\}\) are assumed to be determined by sorting a set of of \(i = \{1, ..., n\}\) drawings of a random variable \(z_{i}\) in descending order. In this thesis, we use the fifth case of the model as described in (Thurstone 1927) to define the following: \[\begin{gather*} z_{i} \sim N(\mu_i ,\ \sigma^2) \end{gather*}\] where \(\mu_i\) is a latent quality variable for object \(o_i \in \mathcal{O}\) and \(\sigma\) is the noise in the perception of this quality. The noise \(\sigma^2\) is assumed to be the same in the perception of all object. As this model is underdefined, we add the following constraints: \(\sigma^2 = 1\) and \(\mu_n = 0\). A formulation of the likelihood of the parameter \(\mu\) using this constraint is available in (Maydeu-Olivares 2004). We then define the latent quality variable as follows: \[\begin{gather*} \theta_{i}^{\ thurst} = -\mu \end{gather*}\] The final ordering of the objects by sorting the latent quality estimate \(\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}\) in ascending order. In this thesis, we fit the Thurstonian model using an implementation by Revelle (2026).

The Thurstonian model provides an estimate of the latent quality \(\hat\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}\) of the objects from he ranking data. Figure \(\ref{fig:thurst}\) shows the rank estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the objects x25, x19, and x17. The exact latent quality estimates \(\hat\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}\) are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:thurst}\).

Combined Models

Combined models join the likelihood of both scoring and ranking models. We hypothesize that this can improve the accuracy in the prediction of the final ordering as both types of data are taken into account.

Mallows-Binomial

The Mallows-Binomial model is a model based on a combination of the Mallows model for ranking and a Binomial model for scores. While the Mallows model is defined in the previous section (see Section \(\ref{rank_mod}\)), we have yet to define the Binomial model for scores.

The Binomial model for scores assumes that the scores given to the objects are ordinal, equally spaced and can be modelled by a Binomial distribution (Pearce and Erosheva 2022). Reformulated from the perspective of the scoring data, each object has its own Binomial distribution, and the assessors’ scores given to each object are assumed to be independent observations of these distributions. The model can be formulated as follows: \[\begin{gather*} y_i | \pi_i, l \ \sim \ Bin(\pi_i, l) \\ \mathbb{P}[y | \pi_i, l] = \binom{l}{y} \pi_i^x (1-\pi_i)^{l-x} \end{gather*}\] where scores have \(l\) levels and \(\pi_i\) is the underlying quality of an object \(o_i\). The expected score of an object \(o_i\) is \(l \cdot \pi_i\).

Combining the Binomial model with the Mallows model provides the following probability distribution: \[\begin{gather*} \mathbb{P}[R_j, S_j | \pi, \eta] = \frac{\exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*))}{\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)} \times \prod_{i=1}^{n} \binom{l}{y_{ij}} \pi_i^{x_i} (1 - \pi_i)^{l-x} \\ \pi = [\pi_1, ..., \pi_n], \quad R^* = \text{Order}(\pi), \quad \eta > 0 \end{gather*}\]

This model incorporates \(n + 1\) joint observations per assessor, with \(n\) scoring observations and \(1\) ranking observation. It jointly models the ranking and the scoring data by assuming the underlying ranking order \(R^*\) is dependent on the ordering of \(\pi\). Using this method we assume the final ordering of the objects is \(\hat{R}^{*\ mbin}\). In this thesis, we fit the Mallows-Binomial model using an implementation by Pearce (2025). This implementation also includes a bootstrapped uncertainty estimate, which we provide in the results below.

The Mallows-Binomial model provides an estimate of the true underlying ranking \(\hat{R}^{*\ mbin}\) from both the scoring data and the ranking data. This model would accept the objects x17, x25 and x19. The exact ranking values are provided in Table \(\ref{tab:mbin}\).

Comparison Between Models

We compare the six fitted models in two separate ways. First, we provide and compare the final ordering each model provides for the ten best objects. Then, in order to compare how well the models distinguish the latent quality of the objects, we perform Z-scoring on the latent quality variables \(\theta_i^k\). In both the Mallows and Mallows-Binomial model a latent quality variable is not estimated by the model. We therefore perform the Z-scoring on the ranks in the final ordering \(\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{O})\) provided by each model. Discussion

In this thesis, we provide accessible definitions of ranking data and scoring data. We put these definitions into context and present six different models, both frequentist and Bayesian, that can be used to analyse such data. These models are explained in great detail, with both mathematically formal and plain text definitions. We fit these models to a real-world dataset and provide their results, which we compare and discuss. We also provide reproducible code for all models, which can be used to analyse further data of the same type. All of this information provides a solid foundation for further research in analysing ranking and scoring data in the context of grant proposal quality assessment.

A Comparison of Models

Of the six models analysed, none provide the same final ordering of the objects on this dataset. Some of this variability in the results is expected; the models solely analysing the scoring data and the models solely analysing the ranking data are fitted on two different datasets, which, by the data collection procedure, do not necessarily agree with each other. However, even within models modelling the same datasets, the models still do not agree.

Within the models solely analysing scoring data, the mean score model and the hierarchical model for scores only have one difference in their final orderings: the second and third position are exchanged. Upon further inspection of the data, this is due to a very small difference in the estimated proposal quality (less than 1% of the latent qualities’ estimated value), which means these two proposals can be considered equivalent in quality. The reason these model provide so similar results is because they both attempt to estimate the latent quality estimate of objects using a formulation of their mean score. However, the hierarchical model takes more factors into account, such as assessor strictness. Despite, providing the same results on this dataset, we therefore recommend using the hierarchical model for scores over the mean score model.

On the other hand, the models solely analysing ranking data result in many differences in the final orderings. This is due to the models being based on different principles. The Plackett-Luce model estimates the probability of an object being in the first position (Marden 2014). Therefore, it strongly favours objects which consistently obtain middling to high ranks, and disadvantages objects which polarise the assessors and obtain some very high ranks and some very low ranks. The simplified Thurstonian model has the same property as the Plackett-Luce model, although slightly less pronounced. The Mallows model works differently; it attempts to find a consensus within the ranking data. This provides an estimate that, with our ranking data, is similar to the results provided by our scoring data. The reason why these estimates are so similar remain to be investigated. These different properties show that depending on which model the user chooses to analyse their data, they are implicitly favoring some types of objects over others.

The Mallows-Binomial model joins scoring and ranking data to provide the final ordering. Using both these sources of information, the latent quality estimates of the Mallows-Binomial model seem to provide more precise estimates than the hierarchical model. Indeed, the confidence interval bands of the Mallows-Binomial model are smaller than the credible interval bands of the Bayesian hierarchical model. While these two intervals cannot be compared one to one, looking at the results in Figure \(\ref{fig:jags_inla_hier}\) and Figure \(\ref{fig:Mallows_bin_ranks}\), it seems that the Mallows-Binomial better separates the quality of the proposals than the Bayesian hierarchical model. However, despite this advantage, the Mallows-Binomial model still has some limitations. Due to its definition (Pearce and Erosheva 2022), the ranking data only contributes very little to the model in comparison to the scoring data. Indeed, the contribution to the likelihood per assessor is \(n\) observations for the scoring data and \(1\) for the ranking data, in total in our dataset, this represents, \(n*m = 10 *28 = 280\) scoring observations, and only \(m = 10\) observations for the ranking data. By analyzing data with this model, a user implicitly decides that the scoring data is of higher importance than the ranking data.

Throughout these analyses, we show that funding agencies, when choosing a model to analyse the scoring and ranking data, are directly making a decision to advandage or disadvantage some types of proposals. Any decision affecting the assessment of proposal quality should be disclosed (Harman 1998). Therefore, the choice of model, when done blindly and , can be considered unfair. We suggest that funding agencies should not only disclose the evaluation criteria in a call for proposal, but also disclose their data analysis procedure in order to provide a transparent and fair grant allocation.

Paths Towards Joint Modelling

The ranking data and scoring data, while both attempting to assess the quality of objects, provide different information about them. The scoring data provides a general assessment of proposal quality on a given scale (“is this object of high or low quality?”), while the ranking data provides a direct comparative assessment of the proposal quality (“is this object of better quality than that other object?”). These two types of information should, in theory, complement each other, as the ranking information may enable us to distinguish small differences in quality between two object with very similar scores. We propose three different methods to jointly model scores and ranks.

We demonstrated in this thesis how the Mallows-Binomial model already combines scoring and ranking data (Pearce and Erosheva 2022). However, we also show that this model, while incorporating both data types, lends more importance to scoring data than ranking data. In order to create a model that considers both sources of information as equal, one could consider modifying the likelihood function of the Mallows-Binomial model to a weighted likelihood (Markatou et al. 1998). The weight of the ranking observations could then be increased and the weights of the scoring observations decreased. This would jointly model Ranking and scoring data on the same scale, without favouring any data type.

A second approach to joint modelling would be to create a hierarchical model in , containing both a scoring and a ranking component with correlated random effects. A framework to integrate this in already exists: the package by Rustand et al. (2024). However, can only fit models which are latent Gaussian. An implementation in this framework would require either a latent Gaussian model for ranking data, which to our knowledge does not exist, or a transformation of the ranking data into a scale which could then be used in a latent Gaussian model. This transformation could consist in using ranks as scores, which is a polarising subject in literature (Stevens 1946; Mosteller 1977; Velleman and Wilkinson 1993), or could consist in directly using the results of a Plackett-Luce, or Thurstonian model as target variable in a Gaussian model. Using one of these options would enable us to jointly model Ranking and scoring data on the same scale, albeit with an additional transformation step.

A third approach to joint modelling is to use the ranking data to create priors for latent quality variables, which can then be used in the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores. The priors could be created from a generalised normal Thurstonian model, as defined in Annex \(\ref{lik:thurst}\). Unfortunately, no closed form formulation of the likelihood of of the generalised normal Thurstonian model exists, so an simulation would be necessary to estimate these distributions. A Gibbs sampling algorithm to estimate such distributions using a Thurstonian model on ranking data is already available in Yao and Böckenholft (1999). The algorithm and the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores could thus be run sequentially in order to obtain a joint model.

Limitations

This work has several limitations, it was only performed on a single real-world dataset containing complete scoring data and partial ranking data. It is possible that the results we report and the findings we discuss are heavily influenced by the properties of these specific data and are not generalisable. Moreover, the fact that the ranking data is only partial, greatly increases the complexity of joint modelling. In this thesis, we do not discuss methods to deal with this incompleteness. However, we believe that these incomplete ranking data reflect real-world scenarios, where assessors’ times are limited, and this property should be taken into account in further joint modelling of ranks and scores.

In this thesis, we only describe a selection of models relevant for scoring and ranking data. This selection is neither exhaustive nor was it systematically created. Moreover, assessing the preciseness and statistical properties of the estimates provided by such models is a complicated task; different models favour different objects traits, and the decision of which traits are important depends on the real-world application of the model. In order to compare models effectively, the specific real-world objective should be defined before the analysis. Many different metrics may be better suited to assess model quality, and many other models may be better suited for analysing scoring and ranking data as a whole than the ones we use in this thesis.

In the model fitting, we also only select single, specific packages for each model. While we do check whether the results for each model are plausible, we do not check whether the full back-end model implementations are correct. Moreover, in our Bayesian model, we discuss and implementations at length, but other Bayesian posterior distribution estimation software also exist. These software implementations may be better suited for fitting the models discussed in the thesis than the ones we used.

Conclusion & Outlook

We performed the preliminary work necessary for understanding grant proposal panel data containing both scoring and ranking components. We describe six models, and find that, depending on the model selected, certain object traits may be advantaged or disadvantaged in the assessment of quality. We highlight the need for a deliberate model selection, and clear transparent communication already during the call for proposal. Subsequently, we provide paths to potential new models that have yet to be used in this context.

We believe that in the case of grant proposal quality assessment, a “one-size fits all” approach to modelling is not the solution. In the future, we hope it will be possible to provide robust and tailored recommendations of models which fit the exact need, and reflect the exact intentions of funders. In order to obtain these robust recommendations, a thorough analysis of models, on multiple real-world and simulation datasets is still necessary. This thesis is the first step towards this path, but much work remains to be done.

Bayesian Estimations

In frequentist models, we define fixed parameters of interest, and then subsequently define methods to find a most likely value for these parameters using data (usually by using maximum likelihood estimation) (Held and Sabanés Bové 2020). In Bayesian modelling, we also define parameters of interest. However, we assume that these parameters are not fixed, but follow distributions themselves. The focus is shifted from determining the most likely value for each parameter towards determining the most likely distribution for each parameter (Held and Sabanés Bové 2020). In order to determine these distributions we use Bayes’ theorem to obtain: \[\begin{equation} f(\symbf{\beta}| D) \propto f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta}) \label{eq-bayes} \end{equation}\] where \(\symbf{\beta} = \{\beta_1, ..., \beta_q\}\) are the parameters of interest and \(D\) are the data used (see Annex \(\ref{bayest}\) for derivation). We denote \(f(\symbf{\beta}| D)\) the posterior distribution; the distribution of the parameters after having taken the data \(D\) into account, and \(f(\symbf{\beta})\) the prior distribution; our belief about the distribution of the parameters before taking the data into account. The function \(f(D | \symbf{\beta})\) is the likelihood of observing the data given the parameters of interest.

In the hierarchical model for scores (see Section \(\ref{methods}\)), there exists no closed form solution to the operation \(f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta})\). In order to obtain results, we use simulations such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (), or numerical approximation methods such as the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations ().

Bayes’ Theorem

Baye’s theorem is defined as follows: \[\begin{equation*} \mathbb{P}[A|B] = \frac{\mathbb{P}[B|A] * \mathbb{P}[A]}{\mathbb{P}[B]} \end{equation*}\] In order to use it in modelling we reformulate it with \(A\) as the parameters of interest and \(B\) the data we have obtained. We define parameters of interest \(\symbf{\beta} = \{\beta_1, ..., \beta_s\}\) and data \(D\) to obtain: \[\begin{equation*} f(\symbf{\beta}| D) = \frac{f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta})}{f(D)} \end{equation*}\] where \(f(\symbf{\beta}| D)\) is the posterior distribution of \(\symbf{\beta}\), \(f(\symbf{\beta})\) the prior distribution of \(\symbf{\beta}\), \(f(D | \symbf{\beta})\) the likelihood function of our model and \(f(D)\) a normalizing constant. \(f(D)\) is normally estimated through marginalization: \[\begin{equation*} f(D) = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(\symbf{\beta}, D) d\symbf{\beta} = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(D | \symbf{\beta}) f(\symbf{\beta}) d \symbf{\beta} \end{equation*}\] This formulation of \(f(D)\) in most cases does not have a closed form solution (unless the prior is conjugate to the likelihood). To avoid having to estimate it methods to estimate posterior distributions requiring only a proportionality constraint are used. We thus use: \[\begin{equation*} f(\symbf{\beta}| D) \propto f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta}) \end{equation*}\]

Markov Chain Monte Carlo

methods are based on two concepts, Monte Carlo simulations, and Markov chains. This section will first introduce these concepts individually, and then explain how they can be used in combination to estimate a posterior distribution.

Monte Carlo

Monte Carlo simulations are a class of numerical methods that generate a series of random independent samples in order to estimate the solution to complex mathematical problems. These simulations are guaranteed, with a long enough simulation, to converge towards the analytical solution of the problem (Metropolis and Ulam 1949). Converging is defined as progressively approaching the solution of a problem as the number of simulated iterations increases (convergence is furthered discussed later in this annex). In statistics, these simulations are often used to estimate integrals of density or probability mass functions, as well as generating posterior distributions. Naïve Monte Carlo simulations work well on simple problems, but are heavily limited by the dimensionality of the problem they are solving. As dimensionality increases, the number of iterations required for convergence increase exponentially.

Markov Chain

To improve the computational performance of Monte-Carlo simulations, we can use Markov-chains. Markov chains are series of numbers \({x^{(1)}, x^{(2)} , ..., x^{(q)}}\) in which any step \(x^{(q)}\) depends, and only depends on the previous step \(x^{(q-1)}\). By using Markov-chains in our Monte Carlo simulation, we no longer use random independent samples to generate solutions to our problems. This dependant sampling is used to do importance sampling, where important domains of the solution space are sampled more than the rest of the space. This drastically reduces computation time (Metropolis et al. 1953). In order to guarantee convergence of the towards the true solution despite the dependent samples, three more constraints are needed in the definition of the Markov Chain: Irreducibility, Aperiodicity and Detailed balance (Hanada and Matsuura 2022).

NoteDefinitions: Irreducibility, Aperiodicity and Detailed Balance

Markov chain: A series of numbers \({x^{(1)}, x^{(2)} , ..., x^{(q)}}\) in which any step \(x^{(q)}\) depends, and only depends on the previous step \(x^{(q-1)}\).

Irreducibility: A Markov chain is said to be irreducible if there exists a transition between any pair \(x\) and \(x'\) in a finite number of steps \(s\).

Aperiodicity: A Markov chain is said to be aperiodic, if the greatest common divisor of all the possible number of steps \(\symbf{s} = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_q\}\) between \(x\) and itself is 1.

Detailed balance The transition probability \(\mathbb{P}[T(x \rightarrow x')]\) between any \(x\) and \(x'\) satisfy the following property: \[\begin{gather*} \mathbb{P}[x] * \mathbb{P}[T(x \rightarrow x')] = \mathbb{P}[x'] * \mathbb{P}[T(x' \rightarrow x)] \end{gather*}\]

Metropolis-Hasting Algorithm

Markov-chains and Monte Carlo simulations can be combined into the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm (Hastings 1970). This algorithm is the basis of the most used algorithms such as Gibbs sampling and the Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm. In this section, we will first introduce the Metropolis algorithm and extend it to the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm.

The Metropolis algorithm draws samples from a probability distribution \(\mathbb{P}[x]\), provided that a function \(f(x)\) that proportional to its distribution is available. The Markov chain in the Metropolis algorithm is generated as follows.

NoteDefinition: Metropolis algorithm

1. Initiate the Markov chain: Choose a value for \(x^{(1)}\), either randomly, or using prior knowledge about the target distribution.

2. Propose a candidate \(x^{(q+1)}\): Choose a real number \(\Delta x\) randomly and propose \(x^{(q+1)} = x^{(q)} + \Delta x\). The random generation process of \(\Delta x\) must be symmetric around 0 in order to satisfy the irreducibility, aperiodicity and detailed balance conditions of the Markov-chain.

3. Perform the Metropolis test: The candidate \(x^{(q+1)}\) is accepted with a probability: \[\begin{equation*} \text{min}(1, \frac{f(x^{(q+1)})}{f(x^{(q)})}) \end{equation*}\] If the candidate is not accepted a new candidate is proposed using step two of this algorithm.

4. Repeat until convergence: Repeat from step two until the distribution of the samples generated by the Markov-chain converges.

The chain created by this algorithm satisfies irreducibility, aperiodicity and the detailed balance of the Markov chain, and the samples generated are therefore guaranteed to converge towards the target distribution, within a finite amount of iterations (Hanada and Matsuura 2022).

Metropolis-Hasting algorithms extend this algorithm by relaxing the constraint of symmetry around \(0\) in generating new candidates. Instead, we allow candidates to be generated using any arbitrary function, as long as the series generated is still an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain. To still maintain the detailed balanced conditions necessary for the chain to converge the acceptance probability is modified (Hastings 1970). This provides the following algorithm:

NoteDefinition: Metropolis-Hasting algorithm

1. Initiate the Markov chain: Choose a value for \(x^{(1)}\), either randomly, or using prior knowledge about the target distribution.

2. Propose a candidate value: Choose a new candidate value for the chain using an arbitrary function\(f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)})\).

3. Perform the Metropolis test: The candidate \(x^{(q+1)}\) is accepted with a probability: \[\begin{equation*} \text{min}(1, \frac{f(x^{(q+1)}) * f(x^{(q)} | x^{(q+1)}) }{ f(x^{(q)}) * f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)}))} \end{equation*}\] If the candidate is not accepted a new candidate is proposed using step two of this algorithm.

4. Repeat until convergence: Repeat from step two until the distribution of the samples generated by the Markov-chain is converges

The Gibbs sampling algorithm and the HMC algorithm are special cases of the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm with precisely specified candidate value functions \(f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)})\).

Gibbs sampling is a Metropolis-Hasting algorithm used in order to estimate multivariate target distributions. Let us consider a target distribution \(\mathbb{P}[\symbf{x} = {x_1, ..., x_r}]\) with a proportional distribution \(f(\symbf{x})\) (Geman and Geman 1984). In Gibbs sampling, the candidate values \(\symbf{x}^{(q+1)}\) are generated by using sequential conditional distributions: \[\begin{gather} x_a^{(q+1)} \sim f(x_a | x_1^{(q + 1)}, ..., x_{a-1}^{(q + 1)}, x_{a+1}^{(q)}, ..., x_{r}^{(q)}) \label{eq:gibbs} \\ \text{For all } x_r \in \symbf{x} \nonumber \end{gather}\] This model is sequential and updated values at stage \((q+1)\) and values at stage \((q)\) are both incorporated together in order to obtain new candidate values. Trace Plots and Autocorrelation

Trace plots are often used to assess the convergence and quality of a simulation. Trace plots are plots that shows the progression of the sampled parameter (\(y\)-axis) across the Markov chain’s iterations (on the \(x\)-axis). On such plots, we can identify if models converge and the number of iterations taken in order to converge (see Figure \(\ref{fig-trace}\), left). If the parameter is stably oscillating around a parameter value, we consider the model as converged.

As can be seen in Figure \(\ref{fig-trace}\), left, the first iterations of an simulation are heavily correlated with the Markov chain’s starting value. Moreover, even when a Markov-chain converges, the samples it generates are autocorrelated. In order to limit the effect of these correlations on the solution provided by simulations, we use burn-in and thinning (Hanada and Matsuura 2022). Burn-in consists in discarding the samples in the Markov-chain that are deemed correlated with the starting value, to determine the number of samples to discard, trace plots are often used. Thinning is used in an attempt to eliminate the correlation between samples after the burn-in period. It involves only taking a fraction of the observation produced by the Markov chain (for example 1 in 10 or 1 in 20 samples in the chain) in order to estimate the distribution of the parameter of interest. If this skipping jump is longer than the autocorrelation structure, we obtain independent samples. Multiple methods exist to determine the correct thinning size. A complete description of these methods is beyond the scope of this thesis.

Convergence and Diagnostics

In Section 5.3.1 we state that given a long enough chain, a simulation will always converge towards the true analytical solution of a problem. This, while true, is a misleading statement. Indeed, as simulations are limited by computing capabilities, it is not always possible to run a chain until it converges towards the solution of a problem. Moreover, if a model is misspecified there may be no stationary solution in the first place.

There exist multiple methods to check if a model has converged. A first check to see if a simulation has converged, is by running it multiple times with different starting values (in this is denoted as multiple chains). A trace plot to visualise all chains (see Figure \(\ref{fig-trace}\), right) can then be used to check if chains converge to the same solution. However, this is only a visual test, many other statistical diagnostics for convergence exist. A complete description of these methods is beyond the scope of this thesis. However, we will describe the intuition behind three methods we use in the assessment of convergence of the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores.

Gelman and Rubin (1992) propose a \(\hat{R}\) measure (also called potential scale reduction measure) to assess the convergence of simulations. It consists in calculating two measures of variance in the chains generated by a simulation. One measure calculates the variance of the samples generated within each chain, while the other measures the variance of the samples generated by all chains together. If these two measures differ, it is assumed that the individual chains have not yet had enough iterations to range over the stationary distributions. The \(\hat{R}\) measure is a division of both these measures, an \(\hat{R}\) close to one therefore signifies good convergence. A multivariate version of the \(\hat{R}\) measure was later developped by Brooks and Gelman (1998).

The Heidelberger and Welch diagnostic (Heidelberger and Welch 1983) is a two-stage test assessing the convergence of an chain. First, it compares the cumulative distribution of the samples generated by a markov chain to the cumulative distribution of a Brownian bridge (which is assumed to be what a stationary chain should sample like). The Cram'er-von-Misis test is used to test the similarity between the two distributions. In a second change, Heidelberger and Welch propose a precision test in order to assess if the model estimates are precise enough.

The last diagnostic we use in this thesis is the Raftery and Lewis diagnostic (Raftery and Lewis 1970). However, it is not used to estimate convergence, but instead used to estimate the length (number of iterations) of an simulation necessary in order to estimate a quantile \(q\) at an accuracy of \(r\) with a probability \(p\). It is based on an indicator function which counts how many samples generated in a simulation fall within an accuracy \(r\) of \(q\).

Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation

The Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation () is a method to approximate posterior distributions based on the Laplace approximation. It uses the approximation multiple times, in nested iterations, to obtain the marginal posterior distribution of the parameters of a latent Gaussian model. While the full and exact construct of INLA is beyond the scope of this thesis, we offer here a short introduction to the Laplace approximation.

Laplace approximation

The Laplace approximation can be used to numerically approximate a function \(f(x)\) around it’s mode, using a normal distribution (Held and Sabanés Bové 2020). We take: \[\begin{gather*} h(x) = \log(f(x)) \end{gather*}\] and approximate it around the mode \(x_0\) of \(f(x)\) using a 2nd order Taylor expansion: \[\begin{align*} h(x) &\approx h(x_0) + h'(x_0) (x - x_0) + \frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2} \\ &\approx h(x_0) +\frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2} \end{align*}\] which then provides: \[\begin{align*} f(x) &= e^{h(x)} \\ &\approx e^{h(x_0) + \frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2}} \\ &\approx f(x_0) * \underbrace{e^{-\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x-x_0)^2}{-h''(x_0)^{-1}}}}_{\propto N(\mu = x_0, \ \sigma^2 = -h''(x_0)^{-1})} \end{align*}\] This can used to estimate the integral of a function \(f(x)\) which would otherwise be hard to integrate.

Generalisation of the Normally Distributed Thurstonian Model

We modify the normally distributed Thurstonian model defined in Yao and Böckenholt (1999) to define the following Thurstonian model: \[\begin{gather*} z_{i} \sim N(\theta_{i}^{\ thurst},\ \sigma_{i}^2) \end{gather*}\] where \(\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}\) is the latent quality variable for object \(o_i \in \mathcal{O}\) and \(\sigma_{i}\) is the noise in the perception of this quality. This can be formulated in a general setting: \[\begin{gather*} \symbf{Z_j} \sim N(\symbf{\theta},\ \symbf{\Sigma}) \end{gather*}\] The vector of all scores \(\symbf{Z_j} = \{z_1, ..., z_n\}\) drawn by an assessor \(j\) can be defined as an \(n\)-variate normal distribution with mean \(\symbf{\theta} = \{ \theta_1^{\ thurst}, ..., \theta_n^{\ thurst}\}\) and covariance matrix \(\symbf{\Sigma} = diag(\sigma_1, ..., \sigma_n)\).

There exists no closed-form analytical solution to estimate the values of \(\symbf{\theta}\) and \(\symbf{\Sigma}\) from ranking data, an open form solution can be found in Annex \(\ref{lik-thurst}\). However, this estimation procedure is very computationally intensive due to the high amount of integration required to obtain results. To solve this issue, parameter estimation can be done using Gibbs sampling Johnson and Kuhn (2013). To our knowledge, no implementation of this algorithm was available online.

Likelihoods

Plackett-Luce

Silverberg (1980) states that the Plackett-Luce ranking process can be regarded as a series of drawing from a vase. We consider a vase containing an infinite amount of balls of \(n\) colours, each colour associated with an object \(o_i \in \mathcal{O}\). The frequency of each specific ball colour in the vase is proportional to the weights \(W\) assigned to \(O\). We then draw in a series of phases from the vase. In the first phase, the first colour drawn determines the object in first position of the ranking. In each subsequent phase, the first colour drawn that has not already been drawn determines the next objects in the ranking. We perform this procedure until all ball colours (all objects) are drawn.

Using the vase model representation of the Plackett-Luce model, we can state the probability of observing a specific linear order as follows:

\[\begin{gather*} \mathbb{P}[R_j| \mathcal{W}] = \frac{w_{1}}{\sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_i} * \frac{w_{2}}{\sum_{i \neq{1}}^{n}w_i} * ... * \frac{w_{n}}{w_n} \end{gather*}\]

If we consider a set \(\mathcal{O}_x\), the set of objects that have not been ranked yet at stage \(x\) of the model and \(o_{i_x}\) the object selected at stage \(x\) of the model, we can rewrite the likelihood more compactly:

\[\begin{gather*} \mathbb{P}[Rj| \mathcal{W}] = \prod_{x = 1}^{n} \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_x} w_i} \end{gather*}\]

using this, we obtain the log-likelihood provided by one observation:

\[\begin{align*} l(\mathcal{W} | R_j) = \mathbb{P}[R_j | \mathcal{W}] &= \log{\Bigl( \prod_{x = 1}^{n} \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_x} w_i} \Bigr)} = \sum_{x=1}^n \log{\Bigl( \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_x} w_i} \Bigr)} \\ &= \sum_{x=1}^n \Bigl( \log(w_{i_x}) - \log(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_x} w_i) \Bigr) \end{align*}\]

which in turn provides the general log-likelihood function:

\[\begin{gather*} l(\mathcal{W} | R) = \sum_{j = 1}^{m} \Biggl( \sum_{x=1}^n \Bigl( \log(w_{i_x}) - \log(\sum_{i \in \mathcal{O}_x} w_i) \Bigr) \Biggr) \end{gather*}\]

Thurstonian

We first define the model as specified in Yao and Böckenholt (Yao and Böckenholt 1999): \[\begin{gather*} \symbf{Z_j} \sim N(\symbf{ \bar{z}} + \symbf{\theta},\ \symbf{\Sigma}) \end{gather*}\] Using \(\symbf{Z}\), the probability of a specific object \(o_i\) being ranked higher than an other object \(o_l\) in a ranking order can be written as: \[\begin{align} \label{thurst_1} \mathbb{P}[o_i \succ o_l | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}] = \mathbb{P}[z_i \geq z_l] &= \mathbb{P}[z_i - z_l \geq 0]\\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(z_i - z_l) \nonumber \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi (\sigma_i + \sigma_l)}} \exp \bigl(- \frac{(x- (\theta_i-\theta_l))^2}{2(\sigma_i + \sigma_l)^2} \bigr) \nonumber \end{align}\] Moreover, the following property holds: \[\begin{gather} \label{thurst_2} \{o_i \succ o_l\} \wedge \{o_l \succ o_p\} \implies \{o_i \succ o_p\} \end{gather}\] Using equation (\(\ref{thurst_1}\), \(\ref{thurst_2}\)) we can then define the probability of observing a full ranking order \(R_j = [o_1 \succ o_2 \succ ... \succ o_n] \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})\) as: \[\begin{align*} \mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}] &= \mathbb{P}[z_1 \succ z_2 \ \cap \ z_2 \succ z_3 \ \cap \ ... \ \cap z_{n-1} \ \succ \ z_{n}] \\ &= \mathbb{P}[z_1 - z_2 \geq 0] \ \cdot \ \mathbb{P}[z_2 - z_3 \geq 0] \ \cdot \ ... \ \cdot \ \mathbb{P}[z_{n-1} - z_n \geq 0] \\ &= \int_{0}^{\infty} \phi(z_1 - z_2) \cdot \ ... \ \cdot \int_{0}^{\infty} \phi(z_{n-1} - z_{n}) \end{align*}\] which in turn provides the general likelihood function: \[\begin{equation*} L(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma} | R) = \prod_{j=1}^m \mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}] \end{equation*}\] and log-likelihood function \[\begin{equation*} l(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma} | R) = \sum_{j=1}^m \log(\mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}]) \end{equation*}\]

Convergence Diagnostics in the Hierarchical Model for Scores

Convergence diagnostics were performed using the CODA package (Plummer et al. 2006). The posterior distribution of all parameters converged with \(\hat{R} = 1\) point estimates and a multivariate potential scale reduction factors of \(1\). This indicates very good convergence (Brooks and Gelman 1998). The autocorrelation factors of all parameters dropped below \(0.05\) after a lag of six iterations (see Figures \(\ref{fig:acf_1}\) for a selection of autocorrelation factor plots). The thinning parameter of 20 used in the fit therefore seems appropriate. No trace plot showed any sign of abnormal behavior across the four chains (see Figures \(\ref{fig:coda_1}\) and \(\ref{fig:coda_2}\) for a selection of trace plots). The Raftery and Lewis diagnostic (Raftery and Lewis 1970) suggests a minimum of 3746 iterations for a 95% probability of achieving an accuracy of 0.005 in the estimation of the 2.5% quantile. Our model was fitted using 80000 iteration and with a thinning parameter of 20, we therefore consider the number of iteration sufficient. We also tested for the stationarity of the posterior distributions of all parameters on all four chains using Heidelberger’s convergence diagnostic (Heidelberger and Welch 1983). All parameter’s estimated by the model passed the test. Due to the model passing all these tests, and visually showing satisfactory properties in the trace plots, we assume the model successfully converged.

# Abstract {.unnumbered}
\pagenumbering{roman}

Funding agencies rely on expert evaluations to assess the quality of research proposals submitted to a funding call. Most often, experts are asked to score proposals on an ordinal or continuous scale. Some funders instead ask experts to rank the proposals from best to worst, and few funders ask experts to both score and rank proposals. These scoring and ranking data are then statistically analysed by the funders in order to take funding decisions. 

This work formalises the concepts of ranking and scoring data in the context of grant proposal quality assessment. We review and compare six different frequentist and Bayesian methods that can be used in the analysis of scoring and/or ranking data. We apply these methods on a real-world dataset containing both scoring and ranking data collected by the American Institute for Biological sciences. We also present and compare two different methods for posterior distribution estimation in the Bayesian models: the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (\textsc{Inla}), and a Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation (\textsc{Mcmc}).

In the data analysis, each of the six models used provides a different ordering of the grant proposals. The choice of model thus has a direct impact on the assessment of proposal quality, and, depending on the amount of proposals to be funded, a direct impact on the funding decision. The choice of Bayesian posterior distribution estimation method did not yield any difference in the results. However, the \textsc{Inla} implementation is significantly faster to fit than the \textsc{Mcmc} simulation.

We show and discuss that some methods for ranking data favour proposals that consistently obtain good ranks, while others favour proposals which polarise and have both very high or very low ranks. These findings are currently limited to the dataset analysed, and the analyses we performed need to be replicated on simulation data and other real-world data in order to provide robust recommendations. We also propose possible approaches to combine scoring and ranking data in joint models in order to combine the information from both data types. While these joint approaches have yet to be implemented, we hypothesise that they could provide more precise and fair analyses than separate models for scores and ranks. This work highlights the importance of the choice of statistical model in the assessment of grant proposal quality, and provides a solid foundation for further work in this field.

## Grant Proposal Funding

When resources are limited, decisions have to be made on how to allocate them. In research, funding agencies make these decisions by accepting or rejecting grant proposals. Agencies contact panels of experts which individually review, rank or score proposals, the best of which are funded and the rest rejected [ @harman_management_1998 ]. However, there is evidence for bias in this process. For instance, funding for innovative, risky or interdisciplinary research is less likely to be awarded than for conservative and conventional proposals [@guthrie_measuring_2019]. Moreover, evaluations from individual experts are known to be unreliable, even after panel meetings, making it hard to objectively assess which proposals are worth funding [@fogelholm_panel_2012]. Despite these shortcomings, there is no existing alternative to this expert peer review system. To mitigate its downsides, statistical methods can be used to analyse the experts' evaluations in order to ensure fair decision making.

Depending on the assessment system, the experts either make qualitative or quantitative statements about the quality of the proposals. This project will focus on statistical methods to analyse two quantitative expert evaluation systems:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Scoring: Where experts give grades to proposal on either a continuous or ordinal scale.
\item Ranking: Where experts rank all, or a subset, of the proposals from best to worst. 
\end{enumerate}


To evaluate grant proposal qualities from these data, multiple statistical models, both frequentist and Bayesian, have already been developed. In this thesis, we propose a novel unified notation framework to write about these models. Using this framework, we review and reproduce six statistical methods for evaluating scoring data and ranking data. We then compare the results provided by each model and discuss their implications. Finally, we provide multiple paths that can be taken towards creating a joint model combining scoring and ranking data to provide quality estimates. We hypothesise that a joint model would enable a more precise analysis of object quality in comparison to the other models made for this purpose. This would improve the current decision-making process and ultimately allow funding agencies to allocate their resources more fairly.


## Definitions

Many different notations and formulations are used in literature to refer to ranking and scoring data. For clarity, this section defines the notation that we will use in this thesis. It is inspired by Heyard et al. [-@heyard_rethinking_2022] and Xia [-@xia_learning_2019].

### Objects and Assessors

We define objects as the set of items that are ranked and scored by the assessors. Conversely, we define assessors as the people giving scores and rankings to the objects. In this thesis, the objects will be the grant proposals and the assessors the experts on the evaluation panel.

::: {.callout-note title="**Definitions: Objects and Assessors**" }

**Objects**  are a set of $n \ \epsilon \ \mathbb{N}$ items to be ranked or scored by assessors. In this thesis we will denote the set of objects as $\mathcal{O} = \{o_1, ..., o_n\}$, with a subscript $i \ \epsilon \{1,...,n\}$.

**Assessors** are a set of $m \in \mathbb{N}$ experts who give scores and ranks to the objects $\mathcal{O}$.  We will denote the individual assessors with a subscript $j \ \epsilon \{1,...,m\}$.

:::

### Scores 

Scores are obtained by having assessors assign a grade to each object by estimating its quality. Grades are measured on continuous or ordinal scales of different lengths. When scoring, assessors examine each object individually and compare it to their personal representation of an ideal object; their assessment of each object is assumed to be independent from all other objects (see Figure \ref{fig-rankscore}, left). In this thesis, we work with scoring data on a continuous scale and we use the convention that \textbf{low scores represent high quality and high scores represent low quality}. \      

::: {.callout-note title="**Definition: Scores**" }

**Scores** $S_j$ are a set of grades given to all of $\mathcal{O}$ by an assessor $j$. The score given to a specific object $o_i \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{O}$ by a specific assessor $j$ will be written $y_{i,j}$ with $i \ \epsilon \  \{1,...,n\}$ and $j \ \epsilon \ \{1, ..., m\}$.

**Scoring data** $S = \{S_1, ..., S_m\}$ are the observed scores given by all assessors.

:::


### Rankings

In contrast to scores, rankings are obtained by having each assessor directly compare all objects against each other and order them from best to worst. The objects are therefore assessed on a relative scale; the assessment of each object is directly influenced by the quality of all other objects in the objects (see Figure \ref{fig-rankscore}, right). In this thesis, we will use the convention that \textbf{the best rank is the 1st, and then, as the rank ascends, the quality of the objects decreases}. 

::: {.callout-note title="**Definitions: Rankings**" }

**Linear orders** $\mathbf{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})}$ are the set of all possible ranking orders of $\mathcal{O}$. For any pair of objects $o_i$, $o_l$ $\in \mathcal{O}$, we will denote $o_i$ $\succ$ $o_l$ if $o_i$ is preferred to $o_l$.


**Ranking order** $R_j \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$ is the linear order given by a specific assessors $j \ \epsilon \ \{1,...,m\}$ to $\mathcal{O}$. The rank position given to an object $o_i \in \mathcal{O}$ by an assessor $j$ will be denoted $r_{i,j}$.

**Ranking data** $R = \{R_1, ..., R_m\}$ are the observed ranking orders given by all assessors.

### Object Quality and Final Ordering 

The ultimate objective of our modelling will be to select the most appropriate subset of objects for funding, given the available funding amount. To obtain this subset, we will need to make a final ordering of the objects, sorting the objects from best to worst. This is either achieved, in models estimating a latent quality variable, by sorting the latent quality variable in ascending order (lower values of the latent quality variables represent higher object qualities), or, in models which directly calculate a ranking, by taking a model's predicted true underlying ranking. Once the final ordering is achieved, a funding line can be introduced. The funding line represents the highest value of the latent quality (the minimal quality standard) necessary for an object to be funded, or, in the case where only a final ordering is available, the limit rank an object needs to achieve in order to be funded.


::: {.callout-note title="**Definitions: Quality and Final Ordering**" }
**Object latent quality** $\theta^{k}_i$ is the latent quality of an object $o_i$ according to a model $k$.

**Final ordering** $\mathcal{F}_k(\mathcal{O}) \ \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$ is the final ordering of the objects $\mathcal{O}$ according to model $k$.

**Funding line** In the cases where $\theta^{k}_i$ is available, the highest value of the latent quality $\theta^{k}_i$ possible for an object to be funded. In the cases where only  $\mathcal{F}_k(\mathcal{O})$ is available, the limit rank an object needs to reach in order for it to be funded. 
:::


## Modelling Approaches to Scoring Data and Ranking Data

Models for scoring data are very abundant in literature. In general, it is assumed that the scores given to objects follow a given distribution from which parameters can be inferred. By interpreting these parameters, statements can be made about the scoring data (for example, regarding the spread of the scores or the expected values of the scores for specific objects). As ranking data are in a special format, they require extra consideration when being analysed. Consequently, many different formulations and approaches exist to model them. In this section, we will discuss notions of how ranking data can be thought of, described and modelled.

### Pairwise Comparisons and Ranking

Ranking data can be thought of as a series of pairwise comparisons. This approach considers that the assessors simply compare many pairs of objects and generate a ranking from these comparisons [@marden_analyzing_2014]. The ranking data are then modelled through a series of parameters representing the probability of winning these pairwise comparisons. This description of ranking data is used in models such as the Bradley-Terry model [@bradley_rank_1952] and the Plackett-Luce model [@plackett_analysis_1975]. This description of ranks comes at a cost as the number of parameters increases rapidly for larger ranking models. 


### Distance-Based Ranking

In distance-based ranking, we assume that the data have a true underlying ranking, and that the assessors are imperfect judges who attempt to reproduce this ranking [@fligner_distance_1986]. The true ranking is perceived as a ranking order $R^* \ \epsilon \ \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$, and the further the observed ranking from this true ranking, the less likely the ranking is to be present in the data. There are many possible parametrisations of this distance. Using a distance-based model instead of a pairwise comparison model drastically reduces the amount of parameters to estimate when modelling.


### Thurstonian Ranking {#sec-tuhrst}

Thurstonian ranking is another way to model ranking data. As in distance-based ranking, we assume that the assessors are imperfect judges. However, in opposition to distance-based ranking, the ranking data are not assumed to be generated based on a true underlying linear order. Instead, we assume the ranking data to be generated by a series of latent random variables representing each object's quality [@thurstone_law_1927]. According to Thurstonian ranking, each assessor observes a realisation of all the random variables and ranks them from highest to lowest based on their value (see Figure \ref{fig-thurst}).
 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figures/thurst_rank.png}
    \caption{Example of Thurstonian ranking with three objects. The lower the score, the higher the quality. Each assessor observes realisations of the three objects' latent quality distributions. The order of the realisations determines the order in which the assessors rank the objects. In this case, the most common ranking observed will be $o_1 \succ o_2 \succ o_3$. The rankings $o_2 \succ o_1 \succ o_3$ and $o_1 \succ o_3 \succ o_2$ will also commonly be observed, and the other linear orders will very rarely be observed.}
    \label{fig-thurst}
\end{figure}

## Ranking Data

An overview of the ranking data can be found in Figure \ref{fig:rank_expl}. Each assessor reported their prefered top 6 objects. Thus, we obtain information about the ranking on seven levels: ranks one through six, and a rank greater or equal to seven. Assessors were not allowed to consider ties in the positions one to six. The inter-assessor reliability calculated using a two-way, mixed effect, fixed rater ICC is 0.55 (95% CI from 0.33 to 0.8), which signifies that the grades given by the assessors have a moderate level of agreement [@koo_guideline_2016]. 


There are 11 missing data points in the ranking data, five MCI, and six MCAR, which represent $18.3\%$ of the ranking data. The MCAR data are due to an assessor ignoring the ranking task. We remove them from the dataset as they do not provide any information about the object qualities. However, due to the nature of the models we are using, the MCI data are problematic. The models assume that when an object has a missing value, it did not make it into the top six of an assessor. Reformulated, a missing value given by a specific assessor represents an object quality worse than the 6th ranked object according to them. However, in the case of MCI data, the missing values do not provide any information about the quality of the object. Therefore, there is a need for data imputation. 

Upon further inspection of the data, three of the five MCI datapoints in this dataset represent objects that were ranked in position seven or greater by all assessors. Moreover, the two last MCI datapoints, were each only included in one of the nine assessor's top six and given the sixth rank. Given this information, we consider that the MCI datapoints represent object which would have been of worse quality than the assessors top-six's, which removes the need for imputation. This can only be assumed due to the nature of this dataset, and would have to be done differently on other datasets.

## Models using Scoring Data

### Mean Scores {#sec-mean}

A first and straightforward approach proposed by Zehlike et al. [-@zehlike_fairness_2022] to assess the quality of each object is to use the mean of all the scores $y_{ij}$ obtained by an object $o_i$ as an approximation of the object's quality. In this case, the latent quality of an object $\theta_i^{\ mean}$ is estimated as follows:

\begin{gather*}
\bar{y}_i = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^m y_{ij}}{m}, \quad i = 1, ..., n \\
\bar{y} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \bar{y}_i}{n} \\
\hat{\theta}_i^{\ mean} = \bar{y}_i - \bar{y} 
\end{gather*}

Note that, by definition, high quality objects will have negative values of latent quality $\hat{\theta}_i^{\ mean}$. This model relies on many strong assumptions. First, it assumes that each assessor has the exact same scoring habits, and is well calibrated with themselves and the rest of the group (for example, that no assessor is stricter or more lenient than their peers). Second, it also assumes that each object is evaluated completely independently from all other objects. Moreover, unless the number of assessors is extremely large, it cannot be used to provide any reasonable uncertainty estimates in its results [@kaplan_sample_2008]. In this thesis, we fit this model using a homemade \texttt{R} script [@R_core].

The mean scores model provides an estimate of the latent quality $\hat\theta_{i}^{\ mean}$ of the objects from the scoring data. Figure \ref{fig:mm} shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. The mean score model would accept objects x17, x19, and x25. Object x19 and x25 are tied for second place, and the difference in latent quality between the tied second place and the fourth place object is very low. The exact latent quality values for the ten best objects accoding to this model are provided in Table \ref{tab:mm}.

### Hierarchical Model for Scores

To alleviate the assessor calibration assumptions discussed in @sec-mean, Heyard et al. [-@heyard_rethinking_2022] proposed a hierarchical model for scores that includes parameters to model the different scoring behaviors of the assessors. This model still offers a latent object quality variable $\theta_i^{\ hier}$ based on the mean of the scores obtained by each object. We defined a slightly modified version of this model as follows:

\begin{gather*}
y_{ij} | \theta_i^{\ hier}, \lambda_{ij} \sim N(\mu + \theta_i^{\ hier} + \lambda_{j}\ , \tau_y^2) \\
\mu \sim N(0, 100) \quad \quad \theta_i^{\ hier} \sim N(0,  \tau^2_{\theta}) \quad \quad \lambda_{j} \sim N(0, \tau_\lambda^2) \\
\tau_y \sim G(1.5,1.5),  \quad \quad \tau_{\theta} \sim G(1.5,1.5) \quad \quad
\tau_\lambda \sim G(1.5,1.5)
\end{gather*}


With $\mu$ taking the value of the mean score of all objects, and $\lambda_{j}$ being the assessor-specific effect representing the differences in scoring habits and strictness. Given the scores are grades given between one and five, we fix gamma priors for the standard deviations $\tau$ with distributions which lie within plausible values for these parameters, with modes of $1$, and standard deviations of $2/3$. Using this model, we obtain and extract the posterior distribution for the latent quality of each object $\theta_i^{\ hier}$, which we use to provide credible intervals of $\theta_i^{\ hier}$. This model makes use of the funding line, Heyard et al. [-@heyard_rethinking_2022] propose that all proposals that have a 50\% credible interval of $\theta_i^{\ hier}$ crossing the funding line should be considered in a lottery instead of being directly accepted or rejected. 


This model can be fitted using the \texttt{ERforResearch} package [@heyard_2026]. This implementation uses the \texttt{rjags}  [@rjags] framework in order to generate a \textsc{Mcmc} sample of the posterior distributions of $\theta_i^{\ hier}$. To fit the model, we will use both a reimplementation of this model in \texttt{rjags}, and a new implementation of this model using the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation \textsc{Inla} framework using the \texttt{inla} [@rue_approximate_2009]. More information on both these methods can be found in Annex \ref{mcmc} and Annex \ref{inla}. The \texttt{rjags} model was called with 10000 iterations of adaptive sampling, 10000 iteration of burn-in and 80000 iterations of sampling in combination with a thinning of 20. Convergence diagnostics of the \texttt{rjags} model are available in Annex \ref{coda-hier} and the model calls are available in Annex \ref{mod_calls}.


Both fitting methods provide very similar posterior distribution for $\theta_i^{hier}$.  Figure \ref{fig:jags_inla_hier} shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to both the \texttt{rjags} fit and the \texttt{inla} fit. Both fits would suggest a lottery to allocate the funding to three of the four best objects: x17, x25, x19 and x21. The exact latent quality values for the ten best objects according to both these fits are provided in Table \ref{tab:hier_m}. Both model fits provide the same output; the largest discrepancy between the two fitting procedure is of a magnitude of $0.02$. However, the \texttt{inla} model does not require any convergence diagnostic and is thus faster to fit.

## Models using Ranking Data
\label{rank_mod}

### Plackett-Luce

The Plackett-Luce model is a model based on pairwise comparison. It assumes that each object $o_i \in \mathcal{O}$ has a fixed probability of coming in first place when compared with the other objects in the set $\mathcal{O'} = \mathcal{O} \setminus \{o_i\}$ [@plackett_analysis_1975]. This probability is estimated by assigning a weight $w_i \in \mathcal{W}$ for each object $o_i$. The higher the value of the weight, the more preferred the object. The relationship between the probability of a specific object $o_{l}$ coming in first place and the weights $\mathcal{W}$ is defined as follows:

\begin{gather} 
\mathbb{P}[r_{l} = 1| \mathcal{W}] = \frac{w_{l}}{\sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_i} \label{decision-luce}
\end{gather}

The Plackett-Luce model considers each ranking order $R_j$ as a sequence of decisions made using Equation (\ref{decision-luce}). A formulation of the likelihood of this model can be found in Annex \ref{lik-Luce}. In this method, the weights $\mathcal{W}$ are the quality estimates of the objects. In order to follow the convention that lower latent quality estimates represent higher scores, we will transform them as follows:
\begin{gather*}
\theta_i^{\ pl-lu} = (1 - w_i)
\end{gather*}
We then estimate the final ordering of the objects by sorting the weights $\mathcal{W}$ in ascending order. To estimate the weight parameters, we  fit a Plackett-Luce model using an \texttt{R} implementation by Turner et al. [@turner_modelling_2020]. 

The Plackett-Luce model provides an estimate of the latent quality $\hat\theta_{i}^{\ pl-luce}$ of each object from the ranking data. Figure \ref{fig:pl} shows the latent quality estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the three objects; x25, x19, and x21. The difference in latent quality between the third place and the fourth place object is very low. The exact latent quality values are provided in Table \ref{tab:pl}.

### Mallows

The Mallows model is a distance-based ranking method which considers a true underlying ranking order $R^* \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$ and assumes the assessors are imperfect judges trying to reproduce this ranking [@mallows_1957]. The probability of observing a specific ranking order $R_j \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$ is defined as:


\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[R_j| R^* , \eta] = \frac{\exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*))}{\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)} \\
d(R_j, R^* ) = \sum_{i,l = 1}^n \mathbb{1}\{o_i \ \ {\succ}_{R_j}  \ \ o_l \quad \text{and} \quad o_l \ \ {\succ}_{R^*}  \ \ o_i\} \\
\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta) = \sum_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})}  \exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*))
\end{gather*}

where $\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)$ is a normalizing constant, and $d(\cdot,\  \cdot)$ is the Kendall distance (minimum sum of adjacent swaps) between two linear orders. The notation $o_i \ \ {\succ}_{R_j}  \ \ o_l$ indicates that the object $o_i$ is ranked higher than $o_l$ in the true ranking order $R_j$. Conversely, $o_l \ \ {\succ}_{R^*}  \ \ o_i$ indicates that $o_l$ is ranked higher than $o_i$ in the true ranking order $R^*$. The $\eta$ parameter\footnote{In literature $\eta$ is often denoted as $\theta$. In this thesis, we use $\eta$, as $\theta$ is used to denote object quality.} represents a measure of similarity between the objects $\mathcal{O}$. A large $\eta$ implies that the distribution of the ranking data $R$ is closely concentrated around the true underlying rank $R^*$, while an $\eta$ of 0 implies that the distribution of the ranking data $R$ is uniform across the linear orders $\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$. Using this method, the final ordering of the objects $\mathcal{F}_{mlw}(\mathcal{O})$ is $\hat{R^*}$. To fit the Mallows model, we will use an \texttt{R} implementation by Sorensen et al. [-@mallows_2020].

The Mallows model provides an estimate of the true underlying ranking $\hat{R}^{*\ mlw}$ of the objects from the ranking data. Figure \ref{fig:Mallows} shows the rank estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the objects x17, x25, and x19. The exact ranking values are provided in Table \ref{tab:Mallows}.

### Thurstonian Model

In Thurstonian modelling, the ranking order is assumed to be determined by drawing from latent random variables $z_{i}$ (as discussed in @sec-tuhrst). The ranking orders $R_j$ given by the assessors $j = \{1, ..., m\}$ are assumed to be determined by sorting a set of of $i = \{1, ..., n\}$ drawings of a random variable $z_{i}$ in descending order. In this thesis, we use the fifth case of the model as described in [@thurstone_law_1927] to define the following:
\begin{gather*}
z_{i} \sim N(\mu_i ,\ \sigma^2)
\end{gather*}
where $\mu_i$ is a latent quality variable for object $o_i \in \mathcal{O}$ and $\sigma$ is the noise in the perception of this quality. The noise $\sigma^2$ is assumed to be the same in the perception of all object. As this model is underdefined, we add the following constraints: $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $\mu_n = 0$. A  formulation of the likelihood of the parameter $\mu$ using this constraint is available in [@maydeu-olivares_thurstones_2004]. We then define the latent quality variable as follows:
\begin{gather*}
\theta_{i}^{\ thurst} = -\mu
\end{gather*}
The final ordering of the objects by sorting the latent quality estimate $\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}$ in ascending order. In this thesis, we fit the Thurstonian model using an \texttt{R} implementation by Revelle [-@r_psych]. 

The Thurstonian model provides an estimate of the latent quality $\hat\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}$ of the objects from he ranking data. Figure \ref{fig:thurst} shows the rank estimates for the ten best objects according to this model. This model would accept the objects x25, x19, and x17. The exact latent quality estimates $\hat\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}$ are provided in Table \ref{tab:thurst}.

## Combined Models

Combined models join the likelihood of both scoring and ranking models. We hypothesize that this can improve the accuracy in the prediction of the final ordering as both types of data are taken into account. 

### Mallows-Binomial

The Mallows-Binomial model is a model based on a combination of the Mallows model for ranking and a Binomial model for scores. While the Mallows model is defined in the previous section (see Section \ref{rank_mod}), we have yet to define the Binomial model for scores.

The Binomial model for scores assumes that the scores given to the objects are ordinal, equally spaced and can be modelled by a Binomial distribution [@pearce_unified_2022]. Reformulated from the perspective of the scoring data, each object has its own Binomial distribution, and the assessors' scores given to each object are assumed to be independent observations of these distributions. The model can be formulated as follows:
\begin{gather*}
y_i | \pi_i, l \  \sim  \  Bin(\pi_i, l) \\
\mathbb{P}[y | \pi_i, l] = \binom{l}{y} \pi_i^x (1-\pi_i)^{l-x}
\end{gather*}
where scores have $l$ levels and $\pi_i$ is the underlying quality of an object $o_i$. The expected score of an object $o_i$ is $l \cdot \pi_i$.

Combining the Binomial model with the Mallows model provides the following probability distribution:
\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[R_j, S_j | \pi, \eta]  = \frac{\exp(-\eta \ \ d(R_j, R^*))}{\psi_{\mathcal{O}}(\eta)} \times \prod_{i=1}^{n} \binom{l}{y_{ij}} \pi_i^{x_i} (1 - \pi_i)^{l-x} \\
\pi = [\pi_1, ..., \pi_n], \quad R^* = \text{Order}(\pi), \quad \eta > 0
\end{gather*}

This model incorporates $n + 1$ joint observations per assessor, with $n$ scoring observations and $1$ ranking observation. It jointly models the ranking and the scoring data by assuming the underlying ranking order $R^*$ is dependent on the ordering of $\pi$. Using this method we assume the final ordering of the objects is $\hat{R}^{*\ mbin}$. In this thesis, we fit the Mallows-Binomial model using an \texttt{R} implementation by Pearce [-@rankrate]. This implementation also includes a bootstrapped uncertainty estimate, which we provide in the results below.

The Mallows-Binomial model provides an estimate of the true underlying ranking $\hat{R}^{*\ mbin}$ from both the scoring data and the ranking data.  This model would accept the objects x17, x25 and x19. The exact ranking values are provided in Table \ref{tab:mbin}.

## Comparison Between Models

We compare the six fitted models in two separate ways. First, we provide and compare the final ordering each model provides for the ten best objects. Then, in order to compare how well the models distinguish the latent quality of the objects, we perform Z-scoring on the latent quality variables $\theta_i^k$. In both the Mallows and Mallows-Binomial model a latent quality variable is not estimated by the model. We therefore perform the Z-scoring on the ranks in the final ordering $\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{O})$ provided by each model. The formulas used to perform the z-scoring are available in equation (\ref{eq:zsc}). In the context of this thesis, we are interested in the performance of the models in distinguishing the highest quality objects, we therefore perform this Z-scoring uniquely on the top-10 objects according to each model, a visualisation of the z-scoring on all ranks is available in Annex \ref{z-full}.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:zsc}
z_i^{k} =
\begin{cases}
\frac{\theta_{i}^k - \hat{\bar{\theta}}^k}{\hat{\sigma}_{\theta^k}}, \quad
 k = \{mean, \ hier,\ pl\text{-}lu,\ thurst \}\\
\frac{r_{i}^k - \hat{\bar{r}}^k}{\hat{\sigma}_r^k}, \quad 
 k = \{mlw,\ mbin\} 
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\bar{\theta}}^k$ and $\hat{\bar{r}}^k$ are respectively the mean score and mean rank of the ten best objects according to model $k$, and  $\hat{\sigma}_r^k$ and $\hat{\sigma}_{\theta^k}$ are respecively the standard deviation of the scores and ranks of the ten best objects according to model $k$.

# Discussion


In this thesis, we provide accessible definitions of ranking data and scoring data. We put these definitions into context and present six different models, both frequentist and Bayesian, that can be used to analyse such data. These models are explained in great detail, with both mathematically formal and plain text definitions. We fit these models to a real-world dataset and provide their results, which we compare and discuss. We also provide reproducible code for all  models, which can be used to analyse further data of the same type. All of this information provides a solid foundation for further research in analysing ranking and scoring data in the context of grant proposal quality assessment.

## A Comparison of Models

Of the six models analysed, none provide the same final ordering of the objects on this dataset. Some of this variability in the results is expected; the models solely analysing the scoring data and the models solely analysing the ranking data are fitted on two different datasets, which, by the data collection procedure, do not necessarily agree with each other. However, even within models modelling the same datasets, the models still do not agree.

Within the models solely analysing scoring data, the mean score model and the hierarchical model for scores only have one difference in their final orderings: the second and third position are exchanged. Upon further inspection of the data, this is due to a very small difference in the estimated proposal quality (less than 1% of the latent qualities' estimated value), which means these two proposals can be considered equivalent in quality. The reason these model provide so similar results is because they both attempt to estimate the latent quality estimate of objects using a formulation of their mean score. However, the hierarchical model takes more factors into account, such as assessor strictness. Despite, providing the same results on this dataset, we therefore recommend using the hierarchical model for scores over the mean score model. 

On the other hand, the models solely analysing ranking data result in many differences in the final orderings. This is due to the models being based on different principles. The Plackett-Luce model estimates the probability of an object being in the first position [ @marden_analyzing_2014]. Therefore, it strongly favours objects which consistently obtain middling to high ranks, and disadvantages objects which polarise the assessors and obtain some very high ranks and some very low ranks. The simplified Thurstonian model has the same property as the Plackett-Luce model, although slightly less pronounced. The Mallows model works differently; it attempts to find a consensus within the ranking data. This provides an estimate that, with our ranking data, is similar to the results provided by our scoring data. The reason why these estimates are so similar remain to be investigated.   These different properties show that depending on which model the user chooses to analyse their data, they are implicitly favoring some types of objects over others.   

The Mallows-Binomial model joins scoring and ranking data to provide the final ordering. Using both these sources of information, the latent quality estimates of the Mallows-Binomial model seem to provide more precise estimates than the hierarchical model. Indeed, the confidence interval bands of the Mallows-Binomial model are smaller than the credible interval bands of the Bayesian hierarchical model. While these two intervals cannot be compared one to one, looking at the results in Figure \ref{fig:jags_inla_hier} and Figure \ref{fig:Mallows_bin_ranks}, it seems that the Mallows-Binomial better separates the quality of the proposals than the Bayesian hierarchical model. However, despite this advantage, the Mallows-Binomial model still has some limitations. Due to its definition [@pearce_unified_2022], the ranking data only contributes very little to the model in comparison to the scoring data. Indeed, the contribution to the likelihood per assessor is $n$ observations for the scoring data and $1$ for the ranking data, in total in our dataset, this represents, $n*m = 10 *28 = 280$ scoring observations, and only $m = 10$ observations for the ranking data. By analyzing data with this model, a user implicitly decides that the scoring data is of higher importance than the ranking data. 

Throughout these analyses, we show that funding agencies, when choosing a model to analyse the scoring and ranking data, are directly making a decision to advandage or disadvantage some types of proposals. Any decision affecting the assessment of proposal quality should be disclosed [ @harman_management_1998 ]. Therefore, the choice of model, when done blindly and \textit{post hoc}, can be considered unfair. We suggest that funding agencies should not only disclose the evaluation criteria in a call for proposal, but also disclose their data analysis procedure in order to provide a transparent and fair grant allocation.

## Bayesian Model Fitting

In the description of the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores, we explore two frameworks for posterior distribution estimation: Markov-chain Monte-Carlo (\textsc{Mcmc}) using the \texttt{rjags} package, and Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (\textsc{Inla}) using the \texttt{inla} package. We provide a translation of the \texttt{jags} code to fit the hierarchical model for scores provided in Heyard et al. [-@heyard_rethinking_2022] into the \textsc{Inla} framework and show that both approaches provide very similar results. Despite the similar results, there are three important distinctions between both frameworks. First of all, \textsc{Mcmc}, for a correctly specified model, can be shown to converge towards the true posterior distribution of a model [@hanada_mcmc_2022], \textsc{Inla} on the other hand, can only provide an approximation of this distribution [@rue_approximate_2009]. Secondly, \textsc{Mcmc} can be used to fit any solvable Bayesian model [@hanada_mcmc_2022], while \textsc{Inla} is only restricted to latent Gaussian models, a model class which is equivalent to general additive models [@rue_approximate_2009]. Finally, while \textsc{Mcmc} model fitting is a very involved process, the use of \textsc{Inla} is more accessible. Indeed, \textsc{Mcmc} model fitting requires knowledge of package specific syntax, and a significant amount of work and knowledge is required to perform convergence diagnostics [@roy_convergence_2020]. \textsc{Inla} uses syntax very close to base \texttt{R}, and by design, does not require convergence diagnoses. In our case, the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores can be formulated in the \textsc{Inla} framework; we therefore recommend its use over \textsc{Mcmc}.

## Paths Towards Joint Modelling

The ranking data and scoring data, while both attempting to assess the quality of objects, provide different information about them. The scoring data provides a general assessment of proposal quality on a given scale ("is this object of high or low quality?"), while the ranking data provides a direct comparative assessment of the proposal quality ("is this object of better quality than that other object?"). These two types of information should, in theory, complement each other, as the ranking information may enable us to distinguish small differences in quality between two object with very similar scores. We propose three different methods to jointly model scores and ranks. 

We demonstrated in this thesis how the Mallows-Binomial model already combines scoring and ranking data [@pearce_unified_2022]. However, we also show that this model, while incorporating both data types, lends more importance to scoring data than ranking data. In order to create a model that considers both sources of information as equal, one could consider modifying the likelihood function of the Mallows-Binomial model to a weighted likelihood [@markatou_weighted_1998]. The weight of the ranking observations could then be increased and the weights of the scoring observations decreased. This would jointly model Ranking and scoring data on the same scale, without favouring any data type.

A second approach to joint modelling would be to create a hierarchical model in \textsc{Inla}, containing both a scoring and a ranking component with correlated random effects. A framework to integrate this in \texttt{R} already exists: the \texttt{inlajoint} package by Rustand et al. [-@rustand_fast_2024]. However, \textsc{Inla} can only fit models which are latent Gaussian. An implementation in this framework would require either a latent Gaussian model for ranking data, which to our knowledge does not exist, or a transformation of the ranking data into a scale which could then be used in a latent Gaussian model. This transformation could consist in using ranks as scores, which is a polarising subject in literature [@stevens_theory_1946; @mosteller_data_1977; @velleman_nominal_1993], or could consist in directly using the results of a Plackett-Luce, or Thurstonian model as target variable in a Gaussian model. Using one of these options would enable us to jointly model Ranking and scoring data on the same scale, albeit with an additional transformation step.

A third approach to joint modelling is to use the ranking data to create priors for latent quality variables, which can then be used in the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores. The priors could be created from a generalised normal Thurstonian model, as defined in Annex \ref{lik:thurst}. Unfortunately, no closed form formulation of the likelihood of of the generalised normal Thurstonian model exists, so an \textsc{Mcmc} simulation would be necessary to estimate these distributions. A Gibbs sampling algorithm to estimate such distributions using a Thurstonian model on ranking data is already available in Yao and Böckenholft [-@yao_bayesian_1999]. The \textsc{Mcmc} algorithm and the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores could thus be run sequentially in order to obtain a joint model. 

## Limitations

This work has several limitations, it was only performed on a single real-world dataset containing complete scoring data and partial ranking data. It is possible that the results we report and the findings we discuss are heavily influenced by the properties of these specific data and are not generalisable. Moreover, the fact that the ranking data is only partial, greatly increases the complexity of joint modelling. In this thesis, we do not discuss methods to deal with this incompleteness. However, we believe that these incomplete ranking data reflect real-world scenarios, where assessors' times are limited, and this property should be taken into account in further joint modelling of ranks and scores. 

In this thesis, we only describe a selection of models relevant for scoring and ranking data. This selection is neither exhaustive nor was it systematically created. Moreover, assessing the preciseness and statistical properties of the estimates provided by such models is a complicated task; different models favour different objects traits, and the decision of which traits are important depends on the real-world application of the model. In order to compare models effectively, the specific real-world objective should be defined before the analysis. Many different metrics may be better suited to assess model quality, and many other models may be better suited for analysing scoring and ranking data as a whole than the ones we use in this thesis.

## Conclusion & Outlook

We performed the preliminary work necessary for understanding grant proposal panel data containing both scoring and ranking components. We describe six models, and find that, depending on the model selected, certain object traits may be advantaged or disadvantaged in the assessment of quality. We highlight the need for a deliberate model selection, and clear transparent communication already during the call for proposal. Subsequently, we provide paths to potential new models that have yet to be used in this context. 

We believe that in the case of grant proposal quality assessment, a "one-size fits all" approach to modelling is not the solution. In the future, we hope it will be possible to provide robust and tailored recommendations of models which fit the exact need, and reflect the exact intentions of funders. In order to obtain these robust recommendations, a thorough analysis of models, on multiple real-world and simulation datasets is still necessary. This thesis is the first step towards this path, but much work remains to be done.

## Bayesian Estimations

In frequentist models, we define fixed parameters of interest, and then subsequently define methods to find a most likely value for these parameters using data (usually by using maximum likelihood estimation) [@held_likelihood_2020]. In Bayesian modelling, we also define parameters of interest. However, we assume that these parameters are not fixed, but follow distributions themselves. The focus is shifted from determining the most likely value for each parameter towards determining the most likely distribution for each parameter [@held_likelihood_2020]. In order to determine these distributions we use Bayes' theorem to obtain:
\begin{equation}
f(\symbf{\beta}| D) \propto  f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta}) \label{eq-bayes}
\end{equation}
where $\symbf{\beta} = \{\beta_1, ..., \beta_q\}$ are the parameters of interest and $D$ are the data used (see Annex \ref{bayest} for derivation). We denote $f(\symbf{\beta}| D)$ the posterior distribution; the distribution of the parameters after having taken the data $D$ into account, and $f(\symbf{\beta})$ the prior distribution; our belief about the distribution of the parameters before taking the data into account. The function $f(D | \symbf{\beta})$ is the likelihood of observing the data given the parameters of interest.

In the hierarchical model for scores (see Section \ref{methods}), there exists no closed form solution to the operation $f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta})$.  In order to obtain results, we use simulations such as Markov chain Monte Carlo (\textsc{Mcmc}), or numerical approximation methods such as the Integrated Nested Laplace Approximations (\textsc{Inla}).


## Bayes' Theorem {#sec-bayest}
\label{bayest}
Baye's theorem is defined as follows:
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{P}[A|B] = \frac{\mathbb{P}[B|A] * \mathbb{P}[A]}{\mathbb{P}[B]}
\end{equation*}
In order to use it in modelling we reformulate it with $A$ as the parameters of interest and $B$ the data we have obtained. We define parameters of interest $\symbf{\beta} = \{\beta_1, ..., \beta_s\}$ and data $D$ to obtain:
\begin{equation*}
f(\symbf{\beta}| D) =  \frac{f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta})}{f(D)}
\end{equation*}
where $f(\symbf{\beta}| D)$ is the posterior distribution of $\symbf{\beta}$, $f(\symbf{\beta})$ the prior distribution of  $\symbf{\beta}$, $f(D | \symbf{\beta})$ the likelihood function of our model and  $f(D)$ a normalizing constant. $f(D)$ is normally estimated through marginalization:
\begin{equation*}
f(D) = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(\symbf{\beta}, D) d\symbf{\beta} = \int^{\infty}_{-\infty} f(D | \symbf{\beta}) f(\symbf{\beta}) d \symbf{\beta}
\end{equation*}
This formulation of $f(D)$ in most cases does not have a closed form solution (unless the prior is conjugate to the likelihood). To avoid having to estimate it methods to estimate posterior distributions requiring only a proportionality constraint are used. We thus use:
\begin{equation*}
f(\symbf{\beta}| D) \propto  f(D | \symbf{\beta}) * f(\symbf{\beta}) 
\end{equation*}

## Markov Chain Monte Carlo {#mcmc}

\textsc{Mcmc} methods are based on two concepts, Monte Carlo simulations, and Markov chains. This section will first introduce these concepts individually, and then explain how they can be used in combination to estimate a posterior distribution.

### Monte Carlo {#sec-mc}

Monte Carlo simulations are a class of numerical methods that generate a series of random independent samples in order to estimate the solution to complex mathematical problems. These simulations are guaranteed, with a long enough simulation, to converge towards the analytical solution of the problem [@metropolis_monte_1949]. Converging is defined as progressively approaching the solution of a problem as the number of simulated iterations increases (convergence is furthered discussed later in this annex). In statistics, these simulations are often used to estimate integrals of density or probability mass functions, as well as generating posterior distributions. Naïve Monte Carlo simulations work well on simple problems, but are heavily limited by the dimensionality of the problem they are solving. As dimensionality increases, the number of iterations required for convergence increase exponentially.

### Markov Chain

To improve the computational performance of Monte-Carlo simulations, we can use Markov-chains. Markov chains are series of numbers ${x^{(1)}, x^{(2)} , ..., x^{(q)}}$ in which any step $x^{(q)}$ depends, and only depends on the previous step $x^{(q-1)}$. By using Markov-chains in our Monte Carlo simulation, we no longer use random independent samples to generate solutions to our problems. This dependant sampling is used to do importance sampling, where important domains of the solution space are sampled more than the rest of the space. This drastically reduces computation time [@metropolis_equation_1953]. In order to guarantee convergence of the \textsc{Mcmc} towards the true solution despite the dependent samples, three more constraints are needed in the definition of the Markov Chain: Irreducibility, Aperiodicity and Detailed balance [@hanada_mcmc_2022].


::: {.callout-note title="**Definitions: Irreducibility, Aperiodicity and Detailed Balance**" }
**Markov chain**: A series of numbers ${x^{(1)}, x^{(2)} , ..., x^{(q)}}$ in which any step $x^{(q)}$ depends, and only depends on the previous step $x^{(q-1)}$.

**Irreducibility**: A Markov chain is said to be irreducible if there exists a transition between any pair $x$ and $x'$ in a finite number of steps $s$.

**Aperiodicity**: A Markov chain is said to be aperiodic, if the greatest common divisor of all the possible number of steps $\symbf{s} = \{s_1, s_2, ..., s_q\}$ between $x$ and itself is 1.  

**Detailed balance** The transition probability $\mathbb{P}[T(x \rightarrow x')]$ between any $x$ and $x'$ satisfy the following property:
\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[x] * \mathbb{P}[T(x \rightarrow x')] = \mathbb{P}[x'] * \mathbb{P}[T(x' \rightarrow x)]
\end{gather*}

:::

### Metropolis-Hasting Algorithm {#sec-mh}

Markov-chains and Monte Carlo simulations can be combined into the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm [@hastings_monte_1970]. This algorithm is the basis of the most used \textsc{Mcmc} algorithms such as Gibbs sampling and the Hybrid Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm. In this section, we will first introduce the Metropolis algorithm and extend it to the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm. 

The Metropolis algorithm draws samples from a probability distribution $\mathbb{P}[x]$, provided that a function $f(x)$ that proportional to its distribution is available. The Markov chain in the Metropolis algorithm is generated as follows.

::: {.callout-note title="**Definition: Metropolis algorithm**" }

**1. Initiate the Markov chain**: Choose a value for $x^{(1)}$, either randomly, or using prior knowledge about the target distribution. 

**2. Propose a candidate $x^{(q+1)}$**: Choose a real number $\Delta x$ randomly and propose $x^{(q+1)} = x^{(q)} + \Delta x$. The random generation process of $\Delta x$ must be symmetric around 0 in order to satisfy the irreducibility, aperiodicity and detailed balance conditions of the Markov-chain. 

**3. Perform the Metropolis test**: The candidate $x^{(q+1)}$ is accepted with a probability:
\begin{equation*}
\text{min}(1, \frac{f(x^{(q+1)})}{f(x^{(q)})})
\end{equation*}
If the candidate is not accepted a new candidate is proposed using step two of this algorithm.

**4. Repeat until convergence**: Repeat from step two until the distribution of the samples generated by the Markov-chain converges.

:::

The chain created by this algorithm satisfies irreducibility, aperiodicity and the detailed balance of the Markov chain, and the samples generated are therefore guaranteed to converge towards the target distribution, within a finite amount of iterations [@hanada_mcmc_2022]. 

Metropolis-Hasting algorithms extend this algorithm by relaxing the constraint of symmetry around $0$ in generating new candidates. Instead, we allow candidates to be generated using any arbitrary function, as long as the series generated is still an irreducible and aperiodic Markov chain. To still maintain the detailed balanced conditions necessary for the chain to converge the acceptance probability is modified [@hastings_monte_1970]. This provides the following algorithm:

::: {.callout-note title="**Definition: Metropolis-Hasting algorithm**" }

**1. Initiate the Markov chain**: Choose a value for $x^{(1)}$, either randomly, or using prior knowledge about the target distribution. 

**2. Propose a candidate value**: Choose a new candidate value for the chain using an arbitrary function$f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)})$.

**3. Perform the Metropolis test**: The candidate $x^{(q+1)}$ is accepted with a probability: 
\begin{equation*}
\text{min}(1, \frac{f(x^{(q+1)}) * f(x^{(q)} | x^{(q+1)}) }{ f(x^{(q)}) * f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)}))}
\end{equation*}
If the candidate is not accepted a new candidate is proposed using step two of this algorithm.

**4. Repeat until convergence**: Repeat from step two until the distribution of the samples generated by the Markov-chain is converges

:::
The Gibbs sampling algorithm and the HMC algorithm are special cases of the Metropolis-Hasting algorithm with precisely specified candidate value functions $f(x^{(q+1)} | x^{(q)})$.


### Gibbs Sampling {#sec-gibbs}

Gibbs sampling is a Metropolis-Hasting algorithm used in order to estimate multivariate target distributions. Let us consider a target distribution $\mathbb{P}[\symbf{x} = {x_1, ..., x_r}]$ with a proportional distribution $f(\symbf{x})$ [@geman_stochastic_1984]. In Gibbs sampling, the candidate values $\symbf{x}^{(q+1)}$ are generated by using sequential conditional distributions:
\begin{gather}
x_a^{(q+1)} \sim f(x_a | x_1^{(q + 1)}, ..., x_{a-1}^{(q + 1)}, x_{a+1}^{(q)},  ..., x_{r}^{(q)})  \label{eq:gibbs} \\  
\text{For all } x_r \in \symbf{x} \nonumber
\end{gather}
This model is sequential and updated values at stage $(q+1)$ and values at stage $(q)$ are both incorporated together in order to obtain new candidate values. Moreover, the distribution of $x_a^{(q+1)}$ depends on all other variable $x_{-a}$ but not on $x_a^{(q)}$ itself.  Using such a generation process for $\symbf{x}^{(q+1)}$ provides the following Metropolis test in the Gibbs sampling algorithm:
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{P}[\text{acceptance}] = \frac{f(x_r^{(q+1)}) * f(x_r^{(q)}) | x_r^{(q + 1)}) }{ f(x_r^{(q)}) * f(x_r^{(q+1)} | x_r^{(q)})}
\end{equation*}
and by definition:
\begin{equation*}
\quad x_r^{(q+1)} \independent x_r^{(q)}
\end{equation*}
thus, 
\begin{equation*}
\mathbb{P}[\text{acceptance}] = \frac{f(x_r^{(q+1)}) * f(x_r^{(q)}) }{ f(x_r^{(q)} * f(x_r^{(q+1)}) } = 1
\end{equation*}

Thus, the Metroopolis test is not needed in order to obtain the distribution $\mathbb{P}[\symbf{x}]$. Gibbs sampling was originally defined by Stuart and Donald Geman [-@geman_stochastic_1984]. However, we use here a simpler definition as formulated in Hanada and Matsuura [-@hanada_mcmc_2022]:


::: {.callout-note title="**Definition: Gibbs sampling algorithm **" }

**1. Initiate the Markov chain**: Choose a value for $\symbf{x^{(1)}} = \{ x_1^{(1)}, ... x_q^{1}\}$, either randomly, or using prior knowledge about the target distribution. 

**2. Propose a new value for each $\symbf{x}^{(q+1)}$**: Using the procedure described in equation (\ref{eq:gibbs})

**3. Repeat until convergence**: Repeat from step two until the distribution of the samples generated by the Markov-chain is converges.

:::

### MCMC for Posterior Distribution Estimation

All the definitions in this section were described as estimating a distribution $\mathbb{P}[x]$ given an arbitrary proportional function $f(x)$. To obtain the posterior distribution as described in Equation (\ref{eq-bayes}). We reformulate the \textsc{Mcmc} algorithm as follows:
\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[x] \propto f(x)  \\
f(\symbf{\beta} | D) \propto f(D | \symbf{\beta}) \cdot f(\symbf{\beta})
\end{gather*}
Thus, we can sample from a likelihood $f(D | \symbf{\beta})$ and a prior distribution $f(\symbf{\beta})$, in order to estimate a posterior distribution $f(\symbf{\beta}| D)$.  


### Trace Plots and Autocorrelation

Trace plots are often used to assess the convergence and quality of a \textsc{Mcmc} simulation. Trace plots are plots that shows the progression of the sampled parameter ($y$-axis) across the Markov chain's iterations (on the $x$-axis). On such plots, we can identify if models converge and the number of iterations taken in order to converge (see Figure \ref{fig-trace}, left). If the parameter is stably oscillating around a parameter value, we consider the model as converged.


As can be seen in Figure \ref{fig-trace}, left, the first iterations of an \textsc{Mcmc} simulation are heavily correlated with the Markov chain's starting value. Moreover, even when a Markov-chain converges, the samples it generates are autocorrelated. In order to limit the effect of these correlations on the solution provided by \textsc{Mcmc} simulations, we use burn-in and thinning [@hanada_mcmc_2022]. Burn-in consists in discarding the samples in the Markov-chain that are deemed correlated with the starting value, to determine the number of samples to discard, trace plots are often used. Thinning is used in an attempt to eliminate the correlation between samples after the burn-in period. It involves only taking a fraction of the observation produced by the Markov chain (for example 1 in 10 or 1 in 20 samples in the chain) in order to estimate the distribution of the parameter of interest. If this skipping jump is longer than the autocorrelation structure, we obtain independent samples. Multiple methods exist to determine the correct thinning size. A complete description of these methods is beyond the scope of this thesis.


### Convergence and Diagnostics  {#sec-conv}

In @sec-mc we state that given a long enough chain, a \textsc{Mcmc} simulation will always converge towards the true analytical solution of a problem. This, while true, is a misleading statement. Indeed, as simulations are limited by computing capabilities, it is not always possible to run a chain until it converges towards the solution of a problem. Moreover, if a model is misspecified there may be no stationary solution in the first place. 

There exist multiple methods to check if a model has converged. A first check to see if a simulation has converged, is by running it multiple times with different starting values (in \textsc{Mcmc} this is denoted as multiple chains). A trace plot to visualise all chains (see Figure \ref{fig-trace}, right) can then be used to check if chains converge to the same solution. However, this is only a visual test, many other statistical diagnostics for convergence exist.  A complete description of these methods is beyond the scope of this thesis. However, we will describe the intuition behind three methods we use in the assessment of convergence of the Bayesian hierarchical model for scores.

Gelman and Rubin [-@gelman_inference_1992] propose a $\hat{R}$ measure (also called potential scale reduction measure) to assess the convergence of \textsc{Mcmc} simulations. It consists in calculating two measures of variance in the chains generated by a simulation. One measure calculates the variance  of the samples generated within each chain, while the other measures the variance of the samples generated by all chains together. If these two measures differ, it is assumed that the individual chains have not yet had enough iterations to range over the stationary distributions. The $\hat{R}$ measure is a division of both these measures, an $\hat{R}$ close to one therefore signifies good convergence. A multivariate version of the $\hat{R}$ measure was later developped by Brooks and Gelman [-@brooks_general_1998].

The Heidelberger and Welch diagnostic [@heidelberger_simulation_1983] is a two-stage test assessing the convergence of an \textsc{Mcmc} chain. First, it compares the cumulative distribution of the samples generated by a markov chain to the cumulative distribution of a Brownian bridge (which is assumed to be what a stationary \textsc{Mcmc} chain should sample like). The Cram\'er-von-Misis test is used to test the similarity between the two distributions. In a second change, Heidelberger and Welch propose a precision test in order to assess if the model estimates are precise enough.


The last diagnostic we use in this thesis is the Raftery and Lewis diagnostic [@raftery_1970]. However, it is not used to estimate convergence, but instead used to estimate the length (number of iterations) of an \textsc{Mcmc} simulation necessary in order to estimate a quantile $q$ at an accuracy of $r$ with a probability $p$. It is based on an indicator function which counts how many samples generated in a \textsc{Mcmc} simulation fall within an accuracy $r$ of $q$.

## Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation {#inla}

The Integrated Nested Laplace Approximation (\textsc{Inla}) is a method to approximate posterior distributions based on the Laplace approximation. It uses the approximation multiple times, in nested iterations, to obtain the marginal posterior distribution of the parameters of a latent Gaussian model. While the full and exact construct of INLA is beyond the scope of this thesis, we offer here a short introduction to the Laplace approximation.   

### Laplace approximation

The Laplace approximation can be used to numerically approximate a  function $f(x)$ around it's mode, using a normal distribution [@held_likelihood_2020]. We take:
\begin{gather*}
 h(x) = \log(f(x))
\end{gather*}
and approximate it around the mode $x_0$ of $f(x)$ using a 2nd order Taylor expansion:
\begin{align*}
 h(x) &\approx h(x_0) + h'(x_0) (x - x_0) + 
 \frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2} \\
 &\approx h(x_0) +\frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2}
\end{align*}
which then provides:
\begin{align*}
 f(x) &= e^{h(x)}  \\
 &\approx e^{h(x_0) + \frac{h''(x_0) (x - x_0)^2}{2}} \\
 &\approx f(x_0) * \underbrace{e^{-\frac{1}{2} \frac{(x-x_0)^2}{-h''(x_0)^{-1}}}}_{\propto N(\mu = x_0, \ \sigma^2 = -h''(x_0)^{-1})} 
\end{align*}
This can used to estimate the integral of a function $f(x)$ which would otherwise be hard to integrate.


## Generalisation of the Normally Distributed Thurstonian Model

We modify the normally distributed Thurstonian model defined in Yao and Böckenholt [-@yao_bayesian_1999] to define the following Thurstonian model:
\begin{gather*}
z_{i} \sim N(\theta_{i}^{\ thurst},\ \sigma_{i}^2)
\end{gather*}
where $\theta_{i}^{\ thurst}$ is the latent quality variable for object $o_i \in \mathcal{O}$ and $\sigma_{i}$ is the noise in the perception of this quality. This can be formulated in a general setting:
\begin{gather*}
\symbf{Z_j} \sim N(\symbf{\theta},\ \symbf{\Sigma})
\end{gather*}
The vector of all scores $\symbf{Z_j} = \{z_1, ..., z_n\}$  drawn by an assessor $j$ can be defined as an $n$-variate normal distribution with mean $\symbf{\theta} = \{ \theta_1^{\ thurst}, ..., \theta_n^{\ thurst}\}$ and covariance matrix $\symbf{\Sigma} = diag(\sigma_1, ..., \sigma_n)$.


There exists no closed-form analytical solution to estimate the values of $\symbf{\theta}$ and $\symbf{\Sigma}$ from ranking data, an open form solution can be found in Annex \ref{lik-thurst}. However, this estimation procedure is very computationally intensive due to the high amount of integration required to obtain results. To solve this issue, parameter estimation can be done using Gibbs sampling [@yao_bayesian_1999 and @johnson_bayesian_2013]. To our knowledge, no implementation of this algorithm was available online.



## Likelihoods

### Plackett-Luce {#lik-Luce}

Silverberg (1980) states that the Plackett-Luce ranking process can be regarded as a series of drawing from a vase. We consider a vase containing an infinite amount of balls of $n$ colours, each colour associated with an object $o_i \in \mathcal{O}$. The frequency of each specific ball colour in the vase is proportional to the weights $W$ assigned to $O$. We then draw in a series of phases from the vase. In the first phase, the first colour drawn determines the object in first position of the ranking. In each subsequent phase, the first colour drawn that has not already been drawn determines the next objects in the ranking. We perform this procedure until all ball colours (all objects) are drawn.

Using the vase model representation of the Plackett-Luce model, we can state the probability of observing a specific linear order as follows:

\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[R_j| \mathcal{W}] = \frac{w_{1}}{\sum_{i = 1}^{n}w_i}  * \frac{w_{2}}{\sum_{i \neq{1}}^{n}w_i} * ... * \frac{w_{n}}{w_n}
\end{gather*}

If we consider a set $\mathcal{O}_x$, the set of objects that have not been ranked yet at stage $x$ of the model and $o_{i_x}$ the object selected at stage $x$ of the model, we can rewrite the likelihood more compactly:

\begin{gather*}
\mathbb{P}[Rj| \mathcal{W}] = \prod_{x = 1}^{n} \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in  \mathcal{O}_x} w_i}
\end{gather*}

using this, we obtain the log-likelihood provided by one observation:

\begin{align*}
l(\mathcal{W} | R_j) = \mathbb{P}[R_j | \mathcal{W}] &= \log{\Bigl( \prod_{x = 1}^{n} \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in  \mathcal{O}_x} w_i} \Bigr)} = \sum_{x=1}^n \log{\Bigl( \frac{w_{i_x}}{\sum_{i \in  \mathcal{O}_x} w_i} \Bigr)} \\
&= \sum_{x=1}^n \Bigl( \log(w_{i_x}) - \log(\sum_{i \in  \mathcal{O}_x} w_i) \Bigr)
\end{align*}


which in turn provides the general log-likelihood function: 

\begin{gather*}
l(\mathcal{W} | R) = \sum_{j = 1}^{m}  \Biggl( \sum_{x=1}^n \Bigl( \log(w_{i_x}) - \log(\sum_{i \in  \mathcal{O}_x} w_i) \Bigr) \Biggr)
\end{gather*}

### Thurstonian {#lik-thurst}
\label{lik:thurst}

We first define the model as specified in Yao and Böckenholt [@yao_bayesian_1999]:
\begin{gather*}
\symbf{Z_j} \sim N(\symbf{ \bar{z}} + \symbf{\theta},\ \symbf{\Sigma})
\end{gather*}
Using $\symbf{Z}$, the probability of a specific object $o_i$ being ranked higher than an other object $o_l$ in a ranking order can be written as:
\begin{align} \label{thurst_1}
\mathbb{P}[o_i \succ o_l | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}]  = \mathbb{P}[z_i \geq z_l] &= 
\mathbb{P}[z_i - z_l \geq 0]\\
&= \int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(z_i - z_l) \nonumber \\
&= \int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi (\sigma_i + \sigma_l)}} \exp \bigl(- \frac{(x- (\theta_i-\theta_l))^2}{2(\sigma_i + \sigma_l)^2} \bigr) \nonumber
\end{align}
Moreover, the following property holds:
\begin{gather} \label{thurst_2}
\{o_i \succ o_l\} \wedge \{o_l \succ o_p\} \implies \{o_i \succ o_p\}
\end{gather}
Using equation (\ref{thurst_1}, \ref{thurst_2}) we can then define the probability of observing a full ranking order $R_j = [o_1 \succ o_2 \succ ... \succ o_n] \in \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})$ as:
\begin{align*}
\mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}] &=  \mathbb{P}[z_1 \succ z_2 \ \cap \ z_2 \succ z_3 \ \cap \ ... \  \cap z_{n-1} \ \succ \ z_{n}] \\
&= \mathbb{P}[z_1 - z_2 \geq 0] \ \cdot \ \mathbb{P}[z_2 - z_3 \geq 0] \ \cdot \  ... \ \cdot \ \mathbb{P}[z_{n-1} - z_n \geq 0] \\
&= \int_{0}^{\infty} \phi(z_1 - z_2) \cdot \ ... \ \cdot \int_{0}^{\infty} \phi(z_{n-1} - z_{n})
\end{align*}
which in turn provides the general likelihood function: 
\begin{equation*}
L(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma} | R) = \prod_{j=1}^m \mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}]
\end{equation*}
and log-likelihood function
\begin{equation*}
l(\symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma} | R) = \sum_{j=1}^m \log(\mathbb{P}[R_j | \symbf{\theta}, \symbf{\Sigma}])
\end{equation*}

## Convergence Diagnostics in the Hierarchical Model for Scores  {#coda-hier}

Convergence diagnostics were performed using the CODA package [@coda]. The posterior distribution of all parameters converged with $\hat{R} = 1$ point estimates and a multivariate potential scale reduction factors of $1$. This indicates very good convergence [@brooks_general_1998]. The autocorrelation factors of all parameters dropped below $0.05$ after a lag of six iterations (see Figures \ref{fig:acf_1} for a selection of autocorrelation factor plots). The thinning parameter of 20 used in the fit therefore seems appropriate. No trace plot showed any sign of abnormal behavior across the four \textsc{Mcmc} chains (see Figures \ref{fig:coda_1} and \ref{fig:coda_2} for a selection of trace plots). The Raftery and Lewis diagnostic [@raftery_1970] suggests a minimum of 3746 iterations for a 95\% probability of achieving an accuracy of 0.005 in the estimation of the 2.5\% quantile. Our model was fitted using 80000 iteration and with a thinning parameter of 20, we therefore consider the number of iteration sufficient. We also tested for the stationarity of the posterior distributions of all parameters on all four chains using Heidelberger's convergence diagnostic [@heidelberger_simulation_1983]. All parameter's estimated by the model passed the test. Due to the model passing all these tests, and visually showing satisfactory properties in the trace plots, we assume the model successfully converged.

## Model Calls for JAGS and INLA

\label{mod_calls}

In this section we provide the models called in JAGS and INLA of the Bayesian hierarchical models for scores.

```{R}
#| echo: true
#| eval: false


### rjags call #############################################################

model_string <- '
  model  {
  
  # Likelihood -------------------------------------------------------------
    for (i in 1:n){
      score[i] ~ dnorm(mu[i], tau_general)
      mu[i] <- overall_m + id_quality[object_id[i]] + ass_effect[assessor[i]]
    }
  
  # Priors for id and ass effect -------------------------------------------
    for(j in 1:n_object){
      id_quality[j] ~ dnorm (0, tau_object)
    }
    for(k in 1:n_assessor){
      ass_effect[k] ~ dnorm(0, tau_ass)
    }
  
  # Prior for overall_m (low information) ----------------------------------
    overall_m ~ dnorm(0, 10^(-3))
    
  # Hyperpriors ------------------------------------------------------------
    sigma_g ~ dgamma(1.5,1.5)
    tau_general <- 1 / (sigma_g^2) # Matched with Inla Priors
    
    sigma_o ~ dgamma(1.5,1.5)
    tau_object <- 1 / (sigma_o^2) #  Matched with Inla Priors
  
    sigma_ass ~ dgamma(1.5,1.5)
    tau_ass <- 1 / (sigma_ass^2) #  Matched with Inla Priors
    
  }
'
# Model defintion
model <- jags.model(textConnection(model_string),
                    data = jags_dat,
                    n.chains = 4,
                    n.adapt = 10000)

# Burn in
update(model, n.iter = 10000)

# Sampling
fit <- coda.samples(model = model,
                    variable.names = c("overall_m",
                                       "id_quality",
                                       "ass_effect",
                                       "sigma_g", "sigma_o", "sigma_ass"),
                    n.iter = 80000,
                    thin = 20)

### INLA call #############################################################

# According to INLA documentation "loggamma" is the gamma prior
prec.prior <- list(prec = list(prior = "loggamma", param = c(1.5, 1.5)))

# Model call
fit_inla <- 
  inla(formula = value ~ 1 + 
                 f(grant_id, model = "iid", hyper = prec.prior) +
                 f(RandomRevID, model = "iid", hyper = prec.prior), 
       data = dat, quantiles = c(0.25,0.75))
```


## Z-scoring on Entire Dataset
\label{z-full}

Figure \ref{fig:zscore_whole} provides an overview of the Z-scores of all objects. This graph is to be interpreted with great caution. Despite the ranking models seemingly distinguishing the top 11 proposals better from one another than the scoring models, in reality it is not the case. Due to the fact that the data we use only provides partial ranking, the z-scoring on the ranking models only take the top-11 objects into account in the standardisation process. This artificially inflates the difference in quality between the objects when compared to the models which take all objects into account for the standardisation.
